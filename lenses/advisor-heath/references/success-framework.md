# SUCCESs Framework Reference
**Source:** Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die (Chip Heath & Dan Heath, 2007)

> "Sticky = understandable, memorable, and effective in changing thought or behavior."

The SUCCESs framework identifies six principles that make ideas stick: **S**imple, **U**nexpected, **C**oncrete, **C**redible, **E**motional, **S**tories. These principles function as a checklist, not an equation --- not every sticky idea needs all six, but the more principles it embodies, the stickier it becomes.

The archvillain opposing all six principles is the **Curse of Knowledge** --- once you know something, you find it hard to imagine not knowing it, which makes communication difficult.

---

## 1. SIMPLE

### [MTS:S] Simple --- Finding and Sharing the Core
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** Simple means finding the essential core of an idea and expressing it compactly. It is NOT about dumbing down or sound bites. It is about forced prioritization --- stripping an idea to its most critical essence, then translating that core into a compact, profound message. "Simple = core + compact." The ideal is the proverb: short sentences drawn from long experience.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: communicating strategy, mission, priorities; need to align behavior across many people; audience must make independent decisions based on your message
- AVOID WHEN: you're providing a technical manual where completeness is required; the audience explicitly needs detail, not direction
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:U] (make the core counterintuitive), [MTS:C] (make the core tangible), [MTS:St] (embed the core in a story)
**Key Examples:** Southwest Airlines "THE low-fare airline" (Herb Kelleher), Clinton campaign "It's the economy, stupid" (James Carville), "Names, names, and names" (Hoover Adams, Daily Record)
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ask: "If we do nothing else, we must ___"
2. Strip away everything that isn't the single most important thing
3. Express the core as compactly as possible --- aim for proverb-like density
4. Test: Can someone who hears this make correct decisions without further instruction?
**Reversal:** Simplicity without depth is a sound bite, not a proverb. "Goats like sprouts" is compact but not core. Always test for profound compactness, not just brevity.

### [MTS:S.1] Commander's Intent
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** Commander's Intent (CI) is a crisp, plain-talk statement at the top of every military order specifying the plan's goal and desired end-state. It exists because "no plan survives contact with the enemy." CI enables people at all levels to improvise toward the right outcome when circumstances change. Two key questions: "If we do nothing else during tomorrow's mission, we must ___" and "The single most important thing we must do tomorrow is ___."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: coordinating large groups; plans are likely to change; people need to make autonomous decisions; onboarding new team members
- AVOID WHEN: tasks are purely mechanical with no ambiguity
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:C] (make intent concrete like Boeing's Runway 4-22), [MTS:U] (make intent surprising to break complacency)  <!-- privacy-ok: термин из книжного конспекта, не финансы владельца -->
**Key Examples:** "My intent is to have Third Battalion on Hill 4305, cleared of enemy, to protect the flank of Third Brigade." Southwest Airlines: "THE low-fare airline" --- any employee can derive correct decisions. "No plan survives contact with the customer. No lesson plan survives contact with teenagers."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Define the desired end-state, not the step-by-step process
2. Make it singular --- you can't have five Commander's Intents
3. Test: If everything goes wrong, does this statement still guide correct action?
4. Validate: Can a new person make the right call using only this intent?
**Reversal:** CI fails if it's too abstract ("maximize shareholder value") --- it must be concrete enough to inform real decisions. Also fails if you try to have multiple equally-weighted intents.

### [MTS:S.2] Forced Prioritization
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** The hard part of finding the core is not removing bad ideas --- it's weeding out ideas that may be really important but aren't THE most important. "If you say three things, you don't say anything." Smart people gravitate toward complexity because they see nuance and multiple perspectives. Forced prioritization fights this tendency. The inverted pyramid in journalism (lead first, details descending) is a structural model for forced prioritization.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: facing decision paralysis from too many options; audience needs to act in uncertain environments; crafting any key message
- AVOID WHEN: building a comprehensive reference document (not a communication)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] Commander's Intent, [MTS:U] (the unexpected element is often WHAT you chose to prioritize)
**Key Examples:** "It's the economy, stupid" --- Clinton had many issues but Carville forced one priority. Inverted pyramid journalism: the lead is everything. "Don't bury the lead."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. List all the things you want to communicate
2. Rank them ruthlessly --- which ONE matters most?
3. Imagine you can only transmit one sentence before the line goes dead
4. Test: Does your audience suffer from decision paralysis? If yes, you haven't prioritized enough
**Reversal:** Forced prioritization can oversimplify genuinely complex situations where multiple factors truly are equally critical. Use concentric circles (core + next ring + next ring) rather than abandoning nuance entirely.

### [MTS:S.3] Schemas and Analogies
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** Schemas are collections of generic properties stored in memory. To communicate compactly, leverage existing schemas through analogies. "A pomelo is basically a supersized grapefruit with a thick soft rind" beats a detailed description because it "flags" an existing concept and modifies it. Hollywood high-concept pitches work this way: "Alien is Jaws on a spaceship." Generative analogies go further --- they become platforms for novel thinking, like Disney calling employees "cast members" (which generates correct behavior predictions for unscripted situations).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: introducing new concepts; audience lacks domain expertise; need rapid comprehension; need alignment across diverse teams
- AVOID WHEN: the analogy is misleading or the schema doesn't transfer well; the audience already has deep expertise in the domain
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:C] (schemas are inherently concrete), [MTS:U] (modifying a known schema creates productive surprise)
**Key Examples:** "Pomelo = supersized grapefruit." "Alien = Jaws on a spaceship" (directs design decisions worth $100M). Disney "cast members" (generative: generates predictions for costumes, onstage behavior, auditions). Solar system model of atom. Contrast: Subway "sandwich artists" (useless analogy --- Subway doesn't want individual expression).
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify what your audience already knows (their existing schemas)
2. Find the closest existing concept to your new idea
3. State the analogy: "[New thing] is like [Known thing] except [Key difference]"
4. Test generativity: Can people predict correct behavior from the analogy alone?
**Reversal:** Schemas can be "somewhat slow route to the real truth" (electrons don't orbit like planets). But accuracy without accessibility is useless. "If a message can't be used to make predictions or decisions, it is without value, no matter how accurate."

### [MTS:S.4] The Palm Pilot Wood Block (Visual Proverb)
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** Jeff Hawkins fought feature creep for the Palm Pilot by carrying a wooden block the size of the device. Whenever someone suggested a new feature, he'd pull it out and ask where it would fit. The block was a "visual proverb" --- a physical reminder of the core value (simplicity and elegance). The Palm Pilot succeeded "almost because it was defined more in terms of what it was not than in terms of what it was."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: teams are adding features/scope; need constant reminder of constraints; fighting feature creep or scope creep
- AVOID WHEN: the constraint itself is the problem (i.e., you need to expand, not constrain)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] Commander's Intent, [MTS:C] (physical objects are inherently concrete)
**Key Examples:** Palm Pilot wood block. Carville's whiteboard. "When your remote has fifty buttons, you can't change the channel anymore."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify the core constraint or value that keeps getting violated
2. Create a physical or visual artifact that embodies that constraint
3. Make it present in every decision-making moment
4. Use it as a literal touchstone: "Where does this feature fit on the block?"
**Reversal:** Physical artifacts can become fetishes --- the block only works if the underlying strategy is sound. Don't let the metaphor substitute for thinking.

## 2. UNEXPECTED

### [MTS:U] Unexpected --- Surprise + Interest
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** To make ideas stick, first GET attention through surprise, then KEEP attention through interest. Surprise happens when our "guessing machines" (schemas) fail --- it triggers the "surprise brow" (widened eyes, dropped jaw), an emergency override that forces us to pay attention and update our mental models. But surprise alone isn't enough --- it must be "post-dictable" (makes sense in retrospect) and serve the core message. To sustain attention, create curiosity through knowledge gaps. Two levels: (1) schema violation (quick surprise), (2) mystery (extended journey of curiosity).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: your message sounds like common sense; audience is tuning out; need to break through pattern/clutter; message is complex enough to require sustained attention
- AVOID WHEN: the stakes are so high that people are already paying full attention; surprise would trivialize a serious topic
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (make the surprise about the core message), [MTS:C] (concrete details make surprise vivid), [MTS:Cr] (statistics as unexpected comparisons)
**Key Examples:** Karen Wood's comedic flight safety announcement. Enclave minivan crash ad. Nora Ephron's journalism teacher ("The lead is: There will be no school next Thursday"). Nordstrom stories (gift-wrapping Macy's purchases).
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify your core message
2. Figure out what's counterintuitive about it --- what are the unexpected implications?
3. Break the audience's guessing machine along that critical dimension
4. Then help them fix their guessing machine with the correct model
**Reversal:** Gimmicky surprise (wolves attacking band members in dot-com ad) is memorable but worthless if disconnected from the core message. HENSION and BARDLE provide surprise without insight --- frustrating, not sticky.

### [MTS:U.1] Gap Theory of Curiosity
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** George Loewenstein's theory: curiosity happens when we feel a gap in our knowledge. Gaps cause pain --- like an itch we need to scratch. We sit through bad movies because not knowing how they end is more painful than watching them. Key insight: we must OPEN gaps before we CLOSE them. Our tendency is to tell people facts, but first they must realize they need those facts. Techniques: pose a question, point out what someone else knows that they don't, present mysteries with unknown resolutions, challenge them to predict outcomes. Critically, "gaps start with knowledge" --- the more you know, the more you focus on what you DON'T know. So sometimes you must fill in enough knowledge to turn an abyss into a gap.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: audience doesn't yet care about your topic; need to sustain attention over a long message; teaching complex material; delivering presentations
- AVOID WHEN: audience is already deeply curious; the gap is so wide it feels like an abyss (fill context first)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (the gap should relate to the core), [MTS:E] (gaps create emotional engagement), [MTS:St] (stories naturally create "what happens next?" gaps)
**Key Examples:** Saturn's rings mystery (Robert Cialdini --- "dust" but 20 pages of riveting science). News teasers ("Which restaurant has slime in the ice machine?"). Roone Arledge making NCAA football interesting by providing context so viewers cared about the gap. Sony's "pocketable radio." JFK's "man on the moon."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Shift thinking from "What information do I need to convey?" to "What questions do I want my audience to ask?"
2. Highlight specific knowledge they're missing
3. If they lack enough background, provide context first (turn abyss into gap)
4. Sequence information like a treasure map --- drop clues, don't dump data
5. Combat overconfidence by making people commit to predictions first
**Reversal:** Curiosity gaps can backfire if the payoff is disappointing (anti-climactic reveal) or if the gap is so wide that people give up rather than try to close it.

### [MTS:U.2] Breaking the Guessing Machine
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Our schemas are guessing machines that predict what will happen. To get attention, break the pattern --- but break it on a dimension that matters for your core message. "Common sense is the enemy of sticky messages." When messages sound like common sense, they float in one ear and out the other. The danger is that what SOUNDS like common sense often ISN'T. Your job as a communicator is to expose the uncommon sense in your message.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: your idea sounds obvious or commonsensical; people nod but don't change behavior; need to distinguish your message from competitors
- AVOID WHEN: your message truly IS common sense and just needs reinforcement through repetition
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (break schemas about the core), [MTS:Cr] (unexpected statistics or examples), [MTS:St] (stories with turning points)
**Key Examples:** Nordstrom "Nordie who refunded money for tire chains" --- breaks schema of customer service. Southwest: "We won't serve chicken salad even if customers want it" --- breaks schema that companies always serve customers. Hoover Adams: "I'd publish the phone book for names" --- breaks schema of what local news emphasis means.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ask: What does my audience currently EXPECT or ASSUME about this topic?
2. Where is my message different from their assumption?
3. Make people publicly commit to their current assumptions (as Ephron's teacher did)
4. Then reveal the gap between assumption and reality
5. Provide the corrected model
**Reversal:** If you break schemas too aggressively without providing resolution, you create frustration rather than learning. Always pair the break with insight (post-dictability).

### [MTS:U.3] Mystery Structure
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Mysteries sustain attention over extended periods. Robert Cialdini discovered that the best science writing for lay audiences begins with a mystery: "described a state of affairs that seemed to make no sense and then invited the reader into the material as a way of solving the mystery." Mysteries create a need for closure --- the Aha! experience is most satisfying when preceded by the Huh? experience. Robert McKee's screenwriting insight: every scene should be a Turning Point that hooks curiosity ("What will happen next? How will it turn out?").
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: presenting complex material that requires sustained attention (lectures, articles, presentations); need to maintain engagement over 15+ minutes
- AVOID WHEN: you need a quick message (use schema violation instead); the "mystery" would seem contrived or trivial
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:C] (mysteries need concrete details to track), [MTS:E] (emotional investment in resolution), [MTS:St] (mysteries ARE stories)
**Key Examples:** Saturn's rings mystery (sustained interest for 20 pages about dust). Fund-raising presentation structured as mystery ("Why do people under 35 give only 10% of donations?"). Screenplays as models of sustained curiosity.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Start with a puzzle: "How is it possible that X?"
2. Deepen the mystery: "And three different expert groups disagree about why..."
3. Introduce clues sequentially (like a treasure map, not a data dump)
4. Reveal the answer after sufficient investment in the journey
5. Never reveal the mystery before class ends (Cialdini was "pelted with protests" when he tried)
**Reversal:** Forced mysteries feel gimmicky. The mystery must be genuine --- the audience should actually want to know the answer. If they don't care about the question, no structure will save you.

## 3. CONCRETE

### [MTS:C] Concrete --- Sensory Language and Tangibility
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** If you can examine something with your senses, it's concrete. Abstraction makes ideas harder to understand, remember, and coordinate around. "Language is often abstract, but life is not abstract." Concrete ideas are full of sensory details --- ice-filled bathtubs, razors in apples. Naturally sticky ideas (fables, urban legends, proverbs) encode abstract truths in concrete language. "A bird in hand is worth two in the bush" has survived 2,500+ years because it makes a speculative-vs-certain trade-off tangible. "Of the six traits of stickiness, concreteness is perhaps the easiest to embrace. It may also be the most effective."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: always --- concreteness is "the only safe language" when you don't know your audience's expertise level; explaining new concepts; coordinating diverse groups; fighting the Curse of Knowledge
- AVOID WHEN: speaking to deep experts who communicate in shared abstractions (though even experts benefit from concrete examples)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (concrete core is stickier), [MTS:Cr] (concrete details boost credibility), [MTS:E] (concrete = emotional --- easier to feel for specifics than abstractions), [MTS:St] (stories are inherently concrete)
**Key Examples:** Aesop's "sour grapes" (2,500+ years across dozens of languages). "A Nordie ironed a customer's shirt" vs "world-class customer service." Boeing 727: "seat 131 passengers, fly nonstop Miami to NYC, land on Runway 4-22 at LaGuardia." James Grant's packet of salt and sugar for oral rehydration therapy.  <!-- privacy-ok: термин из книжного конспекта, не финансы владельца -->
**Actionable Framework:**
1. For every abstract claim, ask: "Can I see/hear/touch/taste/smell this?"
2. Translate abstractions into specific people doing specific things
3. When introducing new concepts, use the most concrete version possible, even if slightly less "accurate"
4. Use physical props when possible (Grant's salt/sugar packet, Kaplan's leather portfolio)
**Reversal:** Experts crave abstraction and may feel that concreteness is "dumbing down." But "accuracy to the point of uselessness is a symptom of the Curse of Knowledge." The manufacturing floor (concrete) beats the blueprint (abstract) every time.

### [MTS:C.1] Velcro Theory of Memory
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Memory is not like a single filing cabinet --- it's like Velcro. One side has thousands of tiny hooks, the other thousands of tiny loops. The more hooks an idea has, the better it clings to memory. Concrete ideas have more hooks because they engage multiple memory systems simultaneously: visual, auditory, emotional, spatial, kinesthetic. Your childhood home has a "gazillion hooks." A new credit card number has one. Great teachers multiply hooks. Jane Elliott's "brown eyes, blue eyes" simulation put so many hooks into the concept of prejudice that students remembered it vividly 20 years later.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: you need long-term retention; teaching concepts that tend to be forgotten; the idea competes with many other ideas for memory space
- AVOID WHEN: the message is inherently multi-sensory and doesn't need additional hooks
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:E] (emotional hooks are powerful), [MTS:St] (stories naturally create multiple hooks), [MTS:C] (every concrete detail is a hook)
**Key Examples:** Jane Elliott's brown eyes/blue eyes simulation (collars, sneering friends, mirror reflection --- hooks across visual, tactile, emotional, social). "The sight of a friend suddenly sneering at you. The feel of a collar around your neck." Compared with just "learning about prejudice" (abstract, one hook).
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Count the hooks in your current message (visual, auditory, emotional, kinesthetic, social)
2. Add hooks in missing sensory channels
3. Create experiences, not just information (Elliott turned prejudice into an experience)
4. Test: 6 months later, what will people remember --- at best, the lead. Make that lead multi-hooked
**Reversal:** Too many hooks can create sensory overload or distract from the core. The hooks should all point toward the same core message.

### [MTS:C.2] The Nature Conservancy Landscapes
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** When TNC could no longer use "bucks and acres" (buying land outright), they needed to make a more ambiguous strategy concrete. Instead of talking about "2 million acres per year" (abstract, incomprehensible), they talked about 50 named "landscapes" --- contiguous plots with unique environmental features. They named the brown hills east of Silicon Valley the "Mount Hamilton Wilderness." Suddenly other groups started using the name. "We're always laughing now, because we see other people's documents and they're talking about the Mount Hamilton Wilderness. We say, 'You know we made that up.'"
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: your goal is too large/abstract for anyone to grasp; you need to mobilize action from diverse stakeholders; donors/supporters need tangible results
- AVOID WHEN: the abstraction is already well understood and motivating
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (break impossibly large goals into concrete sub-goals), [MTS:E] (named landscapes become "eco-celebrities" people care about)
**Key Examples:** 50 landscapes instead of 2M acres. "Mount Hamilton Wilderness" vs "important area east of Silicon Valley." Saddleback Sam (detailed persona vs "target demographic"). Hamburger Helper's "Fingertips" program (visiting homes vs reading "death binders" of data).
**Actionable Framework:**
1. If your goal is too big to visualize, break it into named, bounded units
2. Give each unit a name, identity, and personality
3. Make "milestone moments" visible and celebratable
4. Test: Can a supporter "walk around on" the result of their contribution?
**Reversal:** Naming and bounding can be arbitrary. Make sure the boundaries are ecologically/strategically meaningful, not just convenient for communication.

### [MTS:C.3] White Things and Mobilizing Knowledge
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** People can list about as many "white things in your refrigerator" as "white things in the world" --- because concreteness mobilizes and focuses the brain. Similarly: "five silly things your child has done" is easier than "five silly things anyone has done in the past decade." Concreteness creates shared "turf" where people can collaborate, bringing their existing knowledge to bear. Jerry Kaplan tossed a leather portfolio on the table at Kleiner Perkins. Partners started brainstorming: "How much memory could fit in this?" The portfolio was "magically transformed from a stationery-store accessory into a symbol of the future of technology."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: you need brainstorming and creative problem-solving; the audience has expertise you want to unlock; pitching to investors, partners, or decision-makers
- AVOID WHEN: you need convergent thinking toward a predetermined answer (concrete props might open too many directions)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (concrete focal point prevents decision paralysis), [MTS:U] (concrete demonstrations can be unexpected)
**Key Examples:** Kaplan's leather portfolio ($4.5M investment). Sony's "pocketable radio" (focused engineering for years). HP/Stone Yamashita "Ferrari family at Disney World" exhibit (6,000 sq ft simulation vs PowerPoint). James Grant's salt/sugar packet with prime ministers.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Create a concrete focal point (prop, prototype, persona, scenario)
2. Present it to your audience and let THEM start generating solutions
3. The audience shifts from reactive/critical to active/creative
4. Capture the energy --- let people "steal your idea" (like Denning at the World Bank)
**Reversal:** The concrete focal point must be representative of the real problem. If it's misleading (wrong constraints, wrong scale), it'll mobilize knowledge in the wrong direction.

## 4. CREDIBLE

### [MTS:Cr] Credible --- Making People Believe
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** Ideas need credibility to be believed. There are three main wellsprings: (1) External credibility --- authorities (experts, celebrities) and antiauthorities (real people with relevant experience). (2) Internal credibility --- vivid details, statistics that illustrate relationships, and examples that pass the Sinatra Test. (3) Audience credibility --- testable credentials that let people verify claims themselves. Barry Marshall couldn't get people to believe bacteria cause ulcers despite being right and having data. Eventually he drank the bacteria himself --- a testable credential. When the obvious wellspring is dry, try a different one.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: audience is skeptical; making counterintuitive claims; asking people to change established behavior; the source isn't inherently authoritative
- AVOID WHEN: you have overwhelming credibility already (though even then, internal credibility helps)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:U] (unexpected credibility sources are more memorable), [MTS:C] (concrete details boost credibility), [MTS:E] (emotional stories from antiauthorities)
**Key Examples:** Barry Marshall drinking H. pylori to prove ulcers are bacterial. Pam Laffin (antismoking antiauthority). "Manheim Research Institute" and "FDA" in flesh-eating banana hoax. Wendy's "Where's the beef?"
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Inventory your credibility sources: What authorities can you cite? What antiauthorities?
2. What vivid details support your core? What statistics illuminate relationships?
3. Does your claim pass the Sinatra Test? Can the audience test it themselves?
4. If one wellspring is dry, try another --- persevere like Marshall
**Reversal:** Authority can backfire in an age of institutional skepticism. Sometimes antiauthorities (Pam Laffin, Dennis from the Doe Fund) are MORE credible than experts because they have no hidden agenda.

### [MTS:Cr.1] Antiauthority
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** Antiauthorities are people whose personal experience, not status, makes them credible. Pam Laffin wasn't a health expert --- she was a 29-year-old smoker with emphysema. Dennis from the Doe Fund wasn't a social worker --- he was a formerly homeless man. "It can be the honesty and trustworthiness of our sources, not their status, that allows them to act as authorities. Sometimes antiauthorities are even better than authorities." The Truth antismoking campaign was 7x more memorable than Philip Morris's "Think. Don't Smoke" --- because it used antiauthority resentment rather than authority lectures.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: audience distrusts institutional authorities; topic involves personal experience; target audience identifies with the antiauthority's background
- AVOID WHEN: the audience specifically requires institutional credentials (legal, regulatory contexts)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:E] (antiauthority stories evoke empathy), [MTS:St] (antiauthority IS a story), [MTS:C] (real person = concrete)
**Key Examples:** Pam Laffin (smoker, not doctor, was the antismoking spokesperson). Dennis from the Doe Fund (formerly homeless man drove potential donors). The Truth campaign (teens + antiauthority rebellion vs. Big Tobacco). Jared Fogle (obese student, not nutritionist, for Subway diet).
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ask: Who has LIVED this problem, not just studied it?
2. Let them tell their own story with authentic details
3. The audience's response: "They have no reason to lie --- they're one of us"
4. Contrast with institutional messaging (Philip Morris's "Think. Don't Smoke" INCREASED smoking 36%)
**Reversal:** Antiauthorities can be dismissed as "anecdotal evidence." Pair with statistics or external credibility when needed. Also, not every antiauthority is credible --- they need genuine relevant experience.

### [MTS:Cr.2] Sinatra Test
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** "If I can make it there, I'll make it anywhere." An example passes the Sinatra Test when one example alone establishes credibility in a domain. If you've got the security contract for Fort Knox, you can compete for any security contract. If you catered a White House function, you can compete for any catering contract. These examples derive power from their concreteness rather than from numbers or authority.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: entering a new market or audience; making a bold claim; need to overcome skepticism about capability
- AVOID WHEN: you don't have a genuinely impressive reference (a weak Sinatra Test example hurts credibility)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:C] (Sinatra examples must be concrete), [MTS:St] (tell the STORY of the Sinatra Test example), [MTS:U] (the impressive credential is often unexpected)
**Key Examples:** Safexpress delivered every Harry Potter book in India to every bookstore by 8 AM on release day --- won a Bollywood distribution contract. Also delivered high school board exams. Bill McDonough's textile factory: 38 chemicals out of 8,000 were safe enough to eat, water coming out was cleaner than Swiss drinking water, costs down 20%.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify your most impressive credential --- the one that, alone, proves your capability
2. Tell the story with vivid details (not "we delivered books" but "every book, every store, by 8 AM, no leaks, no early sales")
3. Choose the credential that resonates most with THIS specific audience (Safexpress used Harry Potter for Bollywood exec, board exams because the exec's brother took them)
4. Test: Does the listener think "If they can do THAT, they can do anything"?
**Reversal:** The Sinatra Test requires genuinely exceptional performance. Don't try to inflate a mediocre example --- the audience will sense the stretch.

### [MTS:Cr.3] Testable Credentials
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** The most powerful credibility source may be the audience itself. Testable credentials invite people to verify a claim for themselves --- "try before you buy" for ideas. Ronald Reagan: "Before you vote, ask yourself if you are better off today than you were four years ago." Wendy's: "Where's the beef?" (implicitly: go compare our burgers yourself). NBA rookie orientation: women the players flirted with revealed they were HIV-positive. The challenge: "See for yourself."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: you want to shift credibility from you to the audience; claim is verifiable; audience is hands-on or skeptical of authority
- AVOID WHEN: the claim can't actually be verified by the audience; the test might backfire (Snapple rumor: "Look at the K in a circle!")
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:U] (the test result itself is often surprising), [MTS:E] (experiencing something yourself creates emotional engagement), [MTS:C] (testing = concrete experience)
**Key Examples:** "Are you better off than 4 years ago?" (Reagan). NBA rookie orientation (women revealed HIV status). "Where's the beef?" (Wendy's). Availability bias exercise (predict: homicide vs suicide, floods vs TB, tornadoes vs asthma --- then learn you're wrong). PCA Emotional Tank exercise (coaches find it easy to insult, hard to encourage).
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Frame your claim as a testable challenge
2. Let the audience discover the truth themselves
3. The self-discovered truth is more convincing than any external authority
4. Beware bait-and-switch: the test must be honest and the conclusion must follow logically from the test
**Reversal:** Testable credentials can be gamed (Snapple's circled K "proved" KKK connection through bait-and-switch). The test must genuinely validate the claim, not just create a misleading impression.

### [MTS:Cr.4] Statistics as Relationships
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** "Statistics are rarely meaningful in and of themselves. Statistics will, and should, almost always be used to illustrate a relationship." Beyond War: 5,000 BBs poured into a metal bucket (each = one nuclear warhead) after a single BB (= Hiroshima). Nobody remembers the number 5,000 --- everyone remembers the terrifying roar. The human-scale principle: translate statistics into tangible comparisons. Covey's workplace statistics mapped onto a soccer team. Movie popcorn: "more saturated fat than a bacon-and-eggs breakfast, Big Mac and fries for lunch, and steak dinner --- combined."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: you have a compelling statistic but it's too abstract to stick; need to make scale tangible; audience lacks intuition for the numbers
- AVOID WHEN: the relationship is already intuitively obvious; the comparison is misleading
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:U] (the comparison itself should be surprising), [MTS:C] (human-scale analogies), [MTS:E] (the relationship evokes emotion, not the number)
**Key Examples:** Nuclear warheads as BBs in a bucket. Movie popcorn = full day of fatty foods. Shark vs deer (deer 300x more deadly). Covey's workplace = soccer team with 4/11 not knowing which goal is theirs. Cisco wireless: "If you can increase productivity by 1-2 minutes a day, you've paid back the cost."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Start with: What RELATIONSHIP does this statistic reveal?
2. Find a comparison on a human scale that illuminates the relationship
3. Choose comparisons that evoke emotion (not B-2 bomber costs, but soft drinks and movies)
4. Use statistics as input (to make up your mind), not output (to win an argument)
**Reversal:** "Ethically challenged people with lots of analytical smarts can make almost any case from a given set of statistics." Use statistics honestly --- to illuminate, not manipulate.

## 5. EMOTIONAL

### [MTS:E] Emotional --- Making People Care
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Belief isn't enough --- for people to act, they have to CARE. Feelings inspire action. The Mother Teresa principle: "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will." Carnegie Mellon study: people donated $2.38 for Rokia (individual girl) vs $1.14 for statistics about African suffering. Worse: combining statistics WITH Rokia's story reduced donations to $1.43 --- thinking analytically made people LESS charitable. The "Analytical Hat" suppresses emotional response. Three strategies for making people care: (1) association, (2) self-interest (including higher Maslow levels), (3) identity.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: audience believes but doesn't act; need to motivate behavior change; competing with apathy; need to overcome analytical resistance
- AVOID WHEN: the emotional appeal would seem manipulative or exploitative; analytical precision is genuinely what's needed
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:C] (individual > mass; concrete > abstract), [MTS:St] (stories are inherently emotional), [MTS:Cr] (antiauthorities evoke empathy)
**Key Examples:** Save the Children: Rokia vs statistics. Truth campaign vs "Think. Don't Smoke." Floyd Lee's Pegasus mess hall ("I am in charge of morale"). "Don't Mess with Texas." "Math is mental weight training."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Focus on individuals, not masses (the Mother Teresa principle)
2. Don't put on the Analytical Hat before asking for emotion
3. Choose from three strategies: association, self-interest (WIIFY), identity
4. Move up Maslow's hierarchy --- people care about transcendence and self-actualization, not just security and esteem
**Reversal:** Emotions can be manipulated. The goal isn't to push buttons like a tearjerker movie --- it's to make people genuinely care about things that matter. Also: combining statistics with emotional appeals can REDUCE emotional response (the Carnegie Mellon finding).

### [MTS:E.1] Association and Semantic Stretch
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** The most basic way to make people care is to associate something they don't care about with something they do. "Relativity" is borrowed to create awe; "unique" is used to create specialness. But overuse dilutes the association --- "semantic stretch" makes terms lose their punch. "Unique" usage up 73% in 20 years while "unusual" declined. The superlatives of one generation fade: groovy, awesome, cool, phat. "Sportsmanship" was stretched until it meant nothing more than "not assaulting a referee." Jim Thompson replaced it with "Honoring the Game" --- a fresh association that made people care again.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: your topic doesn't naturally evoke emotion; need to piggyback on existing emotional associations
- AVOID WHEN: the association is forced, misleading, or overused; the topic already has strong natural emotional resonance
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (choose one powerful association, not many weak ones), [MTS:U] (fresh associations are more powerful than stretched ones)
**Key Examples:** "Sportsmanship" → "Honoring the Game" (fresh term, un-stretched). "Relativity" borrowed for profundity. Technical foul rate dropped from every 15 games to every 52 games after PCA's "Honoring the Game" workshops.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Check if your key terms have been semantically stretched (does anyone still care when they hear this word?)
2. If stretched, find fresh language that taps the same underlying value
3. Test: Does the new term make people feel something the old term no longer evokes?
4. Protect the new term from stretch by using it precisely
**Reversal:** Fresh terms eventually get stretched too --- it's an arms race. The solution is ongoing vigilance and willingness to refresh language.

### [MTS:E.2] Self-Interest and WIIFY
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** "What's In It For You" (WIIFY) should be central to every message. John Caples, greatest mail-order copywriter: "First and foremost, try to get self-interest into every headline." Don't sell drill bits, sell the ability to hang your child's picture. But the tangibility of benefits matters more than their magnitude. "Does imagining make it so?" --- Tempe cable TV study: people who imagined themselves using cable subscribed at 47% vs 20% for those who just heard about benefits. Use "you" not "people." But critical insight: we OVERWEIGHT base self-interest (security, money) for OTHERS while recognizing higher motivations in OURSELVES. "If everyone else is in Maslow's basement, we overlook opportunities to motivate people."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: audience needs motivation to act; benefits are real but not obvious; framing matters
- AVOID WHEN: self-interest appeal would insult the audience's sense of identity (firefighters and the popcorn popper)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:C] (make benefits tangible and imaginable), [MTS:St] (show someone enjoying the benefit in a story)
**Key Examples:** "They laughed when I sat down at the piano" (Caples). Tempe cable TV: imagining > hearing about benefits. Floyd Lee: "I am in charge of morale" (Transcendence, not food service). Bonus framing: people prefer "recognition of importance" for themselves but predict others prefer "down payment on car."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Spell out the WIIFY explicitly --- don't make the audience connect the dots
2. Use "you" language: "You enjoy security" not "People enjoy security"
3. Ask them to IMAGINE themselves experiencing the benefit
4. Move up Maslow: try Aesthetic, Learning, Self-actualization, Transcendence --- not just Security and Esteem
5. Test: Would the firefighter be insulted by this appeal? If so, go higher on Maslow
**Reversal:** Pure self-interest appeal can backfire spectacularly (firefighters and the popcorn popper). Sometimes identity trumps self-interest. Also: "trifling" is how political scientists describe self-interest's effect on political views.

### [MTS:E.3] Identity
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** James March's two models of decision-making: (1) Consequences (rational calculation of costs and benefits) and (2) Identity ("Who am I? What kind of situation is this? What do people like me do in this situation?"). Identity often trumps self-interest. "Don't Mess with Texas" worked because it said "Texans don't litter" --- activating identity, not fear of fines. The enforcement program was abandoned because the identity appeal was sufficient. Group interest often predicts political opinions better than self-interest.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: self-interest is insufficient; the audience has a strong group identity; need sustained behavior change (not just one-time action)
- AVOID WHEN: the identity doesn't exist or is too diffuse; the audience explicitly wants rational analysis
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (identity statements can serve as Commander's Intent), [MTS:St] (stories of identity in action), [MTS:C] (show identity through concrete behaviors)
**Key Examples:** "Don't Mess with Texas" --- Texans don't litter (73% awareness in months, 72% litter reduction in 5 years). Firefighters rejected popcorn popper ("We risk our lives --- we don't need gifts to care about safety"). Floyd Lee's Pegasus identity: "A Pegasus chef is in charge of morale, not food." Edison's "muckers" (identity: we muck around and muck in together).
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify the strongest group identity of your audience
2. Frame your desired behavior as "what people like us do"
3. Use respected insiders (not outsiders) as exemplars --- Texas athletes for Texas identity
4. The celebrity/spokesperson matters only insofar as they represent the identity (Schwarzenegger is macho but not Texan)
5. Test: Would the audience say "People like me do this"?
**Reversal:** Identity appeals can feel manipulative if the identity connection is artificial. The message of "Don't Mess with Texas" worked because the athletes genuinely were Texan. An outsider saying "Texans don't litter" would lack credibility.

### [MTS:E.4] The Three Whys
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** The Curse of Knowledge prevents experts from accessing the emotional core of their own ideas. The "Three Whys" technique (inspired by Toyota's "Five Whys") bypasses this by moving from WHAT you're doing to WHY you're doing it. The Murray Dranoff Duo Piano Foundation said "We exist to protect, preserve, and promote the music of duo piano." After three rounds of "Why?", they arrived at: "The sound of the orchestra but the intimacy of chamber music." Surprise brows went up around the room. "The Curse of Knowledge prevented them from expressing it well."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: your purpose statement sounds dry, institutional, or jargon-filled; audience doesn't care about your mission; you've lost touch with why your work matters
- AVOID WHEN: your "why" is already emotionally compelling
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (the deepest "why" is often the true core), [MTS:U] (the emotional core is often unexpected to the organization itself)
**Key Examples:** "Protect duo piano" → "Why?" → "Not much is being performed anymore" → "Why does that matter?" → "The piano is magnificent... two pianos = sound of orchestra + intimacy of chamber music." IDEO's hospital video (shot from patient's perspective) → "Oh, I never realized..."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. State your purpose/mission
2. Ask "Why does that matter?" --- answer honestly
3. Ask "Why?" again --- go deeper
4. Keep asking until you hit an answer that makes someone outside your organization feel something
5. That's your emotional core --- use it in communication
**Reversal:** Sometimes the "why" chain leads to something too grand or disconnected from daily work. The emotional core must still connect back to what you actually do.

## 6. STORIES

### [MTS:St] Stories --- Simulation and Inspiration
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Stories provide two critical benefits: (1) SIMULATION --- they act as "flight simulators for the brain," showing how to act in context, and (2) INSPIRATION --- they give energy to act. Research shows there's no such thing as a passive audience: when we hear a story, our minds simulate it (tracking spatial relationships, evoking sensory areas, even causing subtle lip movements). Mental simulation alone produces about two-thirds the benefits of actual physical practice. Stories naturally embody most SUCCESs principles: they're almost always Concrete, usually Emotional and Unexpected. The hardest part is ensuring they're Simple --- reflecting the core message.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: need to drive action (not just belief or caring); training people for complex, context-dependent situations; fighting the Curse of Knowledge; need to create buy-in for change
- AVOID WHEN: a simple directive would suffice; the story would seem trivial or forced
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (story must reflect core message), [MTS:U] (stories contain turning points), [MTS:C] (stories are inherently concrete), [MTS:E] (stories evoke empathy)
**Key Examples:** NICU nurse who ignored heart monitor and saved baby. Xerox repairman's E053 code story. Jared Fogle's 245-pound weight loss at Subway. World Bank Zambia health worker story (Stephen Denning's springboard). Alyson Buckman's problem student story.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Don't CREATE stories --- SPOT them. "The world will always produce more great ideas than any single individual."
2. Know the three plot types (Challenge, Connection, Creativity) so you can recognize them
3. When you spot a good story, run with it --- don't tack it on the bulletin board
4. Test: Does this story reflect my core message? Does it provide simulation (how to act) or inspiration (energy to act)?
**Reversal:** Stories without a core message are entertaining but not sticky for your purposes. "It's not enough to tell a great story; the story has to reflect your agenda. You don't want a general lining up his troops before battle to tell a Connection plot story." Also: conference presenters were furious when their stories were extracted from their "edifices" --- the Curse of Knowledge makes experts prefer abstractions over their own stories.

### [MTS:St.1] Challenge Plot
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** The protagonist overcomes formidable obstacles and succeeds. David vs. Goliath. Variations: underdog story, rags-to-riches, triumph of willpower. The key element is that obstacles seem daunting --- Jared at 425 pounds is a Challenge plot; his neighbor losing an inch is not. Challenge plots inspire perseverance and courage. "They make us want to work harder, take on new challenges, overcome obstacles. Somehow, after you've heard about Rose Blumkin postponing her one-hundredth birthday party until an evening when her store was closed, it's easier to clean out your garage."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: launching a new project; need to motivate perseverance; audience faces significant obstacles; kickoff meetings, product launches
- AVOID WHEN: the challenge isn't genuinely daunting (the story will feel trivial); the audience needs comfort/connection more than inspiration to push harder
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:E] (courage and perseverance are powerful emotions), [MTS:C] (concrete obstacles make the challenge tangible), [MTS:Cr] (the protagonist's success IS the credential)
**Key Examples:** Jared Fogle (425 lbs → 180 lbs on Subway diet). Rose Blumkin ($500 furniture business → $100M revenue, still working at 100). American hockey team 1980. Lance Armstrong. Sony's pocketable radio. Rosa Parks.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify the protagonist and their formidable obstacle
2. Show the journey: the struggle, setbacks, and eventual triumph
3. The moral is implicit: "If they can overcome this, so can I"
4. Use at kickoffs, launches, and times when people need energy to persist
**Reversal:** Challenge plots can seem manipulative if the obstacle is artificial or the resolution unearned. Also, a Challenge plot at a company Christmas party is tone-deaf --- use Connection plot instead.

### [MTS:St.2] Connection Plot
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** People develop a relationship that bridges a gap --- racial, class, ethnic, religious, or otherwise. The Good Samaritan is the archetype: a despised Samaritan helps a wounded Jew, crossing a huge social gulf. Connection plots don't require life-and-death stakes --- the Mean Joe Greene/Coke commercial (scrawny white kid gives Coke to towering black athlete) is clearly a Connection plot. "All Connection plots inspire us in social ways. They make us want to help others, be more tolerant, work with others, love others."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: building team cohesion; bridging divides; company social events; need to inspire collaboration and empathy
- AVOID WHEN: the audience needs individual inspiration to push harder (use Challenge plot)
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:E] (empathy and belonging are core emotions), [MTS:C] (the gap being bridged should be specific and visible)
**Key Examples:** Good Samaritan. Mean Joe Greene Coke commercial. Romeo and Juliet. Titanic. Most common plot type in Chicken Soup for the Soul.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify two parties separated by a meaningful gap
2. Show the moment of connection that bridges it
3. The moral is implicit: "We are more alike than different"
4. Use at team-building events, cross-functional meetings, cultural moments
**Reversal:** Connection plots can feel forced if the "gap" is trivial or manufactured. The connection must feel genuine and earned.

### [MTS:St.3] Creativity Plot
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Someone makes a mental breakthrough, solves a long-standing puzzle, or attacks a problem innovatively. The MacGyver plot. Newton's apple. The Ingersoll-Rand "Drag Test" --- when the team needed to decide between plastic and metal casing, they tied samples to a car bumper and drove around a parking lot until police stopped them. "The Drag Test implied, 'We still need to get the right data to make decisions. We just need to do it a lot quicker.'" Creativity plots make us want to "do something different, to be creative, to experiment with new approaches."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: trying to change organizational culture toward innovation; need to inspire creative problem-solving; fighting bureaucratic inertia
- AVOID WHEN: the audience needs to follow established procedures, not improvise
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:U] (the creative breakthrough IS unexpected), [MTS:C] (the creative solution is concrete and tangible)
**Key Examples:** Ingersoll-Rand "Drag Test" (4-year product cycle → 1 year). Shackleton putting complainers in his own tent. Newton's apple.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify the mental breakthrough or innovative approach
2. Show the conventional approach being bypassed
3. The moral is implicit: "There's a better way --- go find it"
4. Use when launching innovation initiatives or celebrating creative solutions
**Reversal:** Creativity plots can be misread as "break all the rules." The Drag Test still required data --- just gathered differently. Make sure the moral includes the constraint ("We still need data --- just faster").

### [MTS:St.4] Springboard Stories
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Stephen Denning's concept: a story that lets people see how an existing problem might change. Springboard stories tell people about possibilities. They combat skepticism and create buy-in by engaging "the little voice inside the head" --- instead of ignoring the inner skeptic, give it something to do. "Tell a story in a way that elicits a second story from the little voice." After Denning told the Zambia health worker story at the World Bank, executives raced up with ideas: "They'd stolen my idea! ... How wonderful! They've stolen my idea. It's become their idea!" Springboard stories are exercises in mass customization --- each listener uses the story as a springboard to a slightly different destination.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: proposing organizational change; fighting institutional resistance; need buy-in from diverse stakeholders; limited time to make a case
- AVOID WHEN: the change is already accepted; detailed analysis is specifically requested
- COMBINES WITH: [MTS:S] (the story must reflect the core change message), [MTS:U] (the possibility itself is unexpected), [MTS:C] (stories are concrete), [MTS:E] (possibility evokes hope)
**Key Examples:** World Bank Zambia health worker (knowledge management became the president's priority). FedEx Purple Promise stories. Costco "salmon stories."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Find a concrete example where the desired change already happened (even partially)
2. Tell that story to the decision-makers
3. Let them generate their OWN implications and next steps
4. The story becomes THEIR idea, not yours --- which is precisely what you want
5. "You can extract a moral from a story, but you can't extract a story from a moral"
**Reversal:** Springboard stories must be TRUE and relevant. A story about another industry or context may not transfer. Also, the story should be brief enough to spark imagination, not so long that it becomes a lecture.

### [MTS:St.5] Spotting Stories
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** "You don't always have to CREATE sticky ideas. Spotting them is often easier and more useful." The Jared story passed through multiple hands before becoming a national campaign: a dormmate wrote an article, a Men's Health reporter included a blurb, a franchise owner spotted it, a creative director followed up, an ad agency president made the spot for free, and finally national Subway embraced it. "How many great ideas have been extinguished because someone in the middle dropped the ball?" To spot stories, know the three plot types (Challenge, Connection, Creativity) and maintain "Core Idea Glasses" --- a deeply ingrained sense of the core message that filters incoming information.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: always --- spotting is more reliable than creating; you need stories that reflect your core message
- AVOID WHEN: you're already drowning in stories and need to curate, not spot
- COMBINES WITH: All other principles (a spotted story can be evaluated against the full SUCCESs checklist)
**Key Examples:** Jared Fogle story spotted by franchise owner Bob Ocwieja. Nordstrom stories spotted and circulated. Costco "salmon stories" circulated until "other buyers come to me and say, 'I've got a salmon story to tell you.'"
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Maintain "Core Idea Glasses" --- always filter incoming information through your core message
2. Know the three plot types so you recognize them when life hands you one
3. When you spot a promising story, ACT --- don't tack it on the bulletin board
4. Create systems for stories to flow upward and outward (like FedEx Purple Promise awards)
5. "In the normal world, a franchise owner would have been amused by the Jared tale. He would have tacked it up on the bulletin board... And that would have been the pinnacle of the Jared tale."
**Reversal:** Not every interesting anecdote is a sticky story. It must reflect the core message and pass the SUCCESs checklist. Also, beware of confirmation bias --- spotting only stories that confirm what you already believe.

## Clinic Transformations (Before/After Patterns)

The book includes "Idea Clinics" that demonstrate the stickiness improvement process:

1. **Sun Exposure** (Ch.1): Lead buried in paragraph 5. Fix: Unbury the lead ("skin damage is cumulative and irreversible"), add aging analogy.
2. **Foreign Aid** (Ch.2): Abstract billions with B-2 bomber comparison. Fix: Break "generous America" schema, replace B-2 with soft drinks and movies.
3. **Fund-raising Presentation** (Ch.2): Summary approach (spoon-feeding facts). Fix: Mystery structure ("Why do people under 35 give only 10%?").
4. **Oral Rehydration Therapy** (Ch.3): Scientific description. Fix: Salt/sugar packet prop with unexpected cost comparison.
5. **Shark Attack Hysteria** (Ch.4): Drowning comparison. Fix: "Shark vs. Deer" --- deer 300x more deadly.
6. **Flawed Intuition** (Ch.4): Statistics about other people being wrong. Fix: Make readers predict FIRST, discover their own errors.
7. **Why Study Algebra** (Ch.5): Expert definitions / base self-interest. Fix: "Math is mental weight training" --- unexpected analogy + higher Maslow.
8. **Problem Students** (Ch.6): Bullet-point tips. Fix: Professor Buckman's story (simulation of dealing with a bully).

## Communication Framework (Epilogue)

For an idea to stick, it must make the audience:

| Goal | Principle |
|---|---|
| **Pay attention** | UNEXPECTED |
| **Understand and remember** | CONCRETE |
| **Agree / Believe** | CREDIBLE |
| **Care** | EMOTIONAL |
| **Be able to act on it** | STORY |

SIMPLE helps throughout the process --- it tells you WHAT to say. All six together form the SUCCESs checklist.
