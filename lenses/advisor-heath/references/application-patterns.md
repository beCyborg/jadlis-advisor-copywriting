# Application Patterns Reference
**Source:** Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die (Chip Heath & Dan Heath, 2007)

This file covers cross-element patterns, the Curse of Knowledge (the central villain), communication frameworks, teaching applications, and the complete sticky ideas checklist.

---

## 1. The Curse of Knowledge --- The Central Villain

### [MTS:CK] Curse of Knowledge
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Introduction + all chapters
**Core Principle:** Once we know something, we find it hard to imagine what it was like not to know it. Our knowledge has "cursed" us. This is the single greatest barrier to making ideas stick. The tapper/listener experiment demonstrates it perfectly: tappers predicted listeners would guess their song 50% of the time; the actual rate was 2.5%. Tappers hear the melody in their heads; listeners hear only disconnected taps. "The tapper/listener experiment is reenacted every day across the world. The tappers and listeners are CEOs and frontline employees, teachers and students, politicians and voters."

**How It Manifests:**
- **Burying the lead** --- experts get lost in details and fail to see what matters most to the audience
- **Abstraction creep** --- engineers make drawings "increasingly elaborate" when manufacturers need them at the machine; experts talk in probability clouds when students need planetary orbits
- **Accuracy fetishism** --- "maximize shareholder value" is more accurate than "THE low-fare airline" but utterly useless for guiding decisions
- **Feature creep** --- "Rather than let it go to waste, what if we give people the ability to toggle between Julian and Gregorian calendars?"
- **Schema blindness** --- the duo piano group says "preserve duo piano music" when they mean "the sound of the orchestra but the intimacy of chamber music"

**Why It's Inevitable:**
The same expertise that helps you arrive at the Answer works against you when Telling Others. "You know things that others don't know, and you can't remember what it was like not to know those things." There are only two ways to beat it: (1) not learn anything, (2) transform your ideas using the SUCCESs checklist.

**Counter-Strategies:**
1. **Use concrete language** [MTS:C] --- "the universal language" that everyone speaks
2. **Use schemas and analogies** [MTS:S.3] --- connect new ideas to what people already know
3. **Tell stories** [MTS:St] --- stories force concreteness and bypass abstraction
4. **Apply the Three Whys** [MTS:E.4] --- drill past jargon to the emotional core
5. **Use the SUCCESs checklist** --- systematically test each principle against your message
6. **Get outsider feedback** --- if someone unfamiliar with your topic can't retell your message, you're still tapping

**Key Examples:**
- Tappers predicted 50% success, achieved 2.5%
- "Maximize shareholder value" vs "THE low-fare airline"
- JFK as CEO: "Our mission is to become the international leader in the space industry through maximum team-centered innovation" vs "Put a man on the moon by the end of the decade"
- Engineers making drawings more elaborate when manufacturers need them at the machine
- Conference presenters furious that their stories were extracted from their "intellectual edifices"
- Duo piano group needing 10 minutes and multiple "why?" questions to find their emotional core

## 2. Cross-Element Patterns

### [MTS:X.1] The Stickiness Multiplier Effect
**Core Principle:** Ideas that embody multiple SUCCESs principles are exponentially stickier. The book's analysis of Jared Fogle (Subway) demonstrates this --- it hits all six: Simple (eat subs, lose weight), Unexpected (lost weight on fast food), Concrete (60-inch pants, specific sandwiches), Credible (antiauthority --- the 425-lb guy giving diet advice), Emotional (individual triumph, Maslow's self-actualization), Story (Challenge plot). By contrast, "7 Under 6" hits only Simple and barely Credible.

**Application Pattern:**
1. Draft your message
2. Score it against all six principles
3. Identify the weakest dimensions
4. Strengthen weak dimensions without sacrificing the core
5. The SUCCESs checklist is a diagnostic tool, not a formula

### [MTS:X.2] The Unexpected-Concrete Combination
**Core Principle:** The most powerful one-two punch in communication. Break the audience's guessing machine (Unexpected) with something they can see, hear, or touch (Concrete). "A bag of popcorn has more saturated fat than a bacon-and-eggs breakfast, Big Mac and fries for lunch, and steak dinner --- combined." The unexpected comparison is what gets attention; the concrete image is what makes it stick.

**Examples:**
- Movie popcorn = full day of fatty foods (laid out on a table for cameras)
- Nuclear warheads as BBs poured into a metal bucket
- Shark vs deer (deer 300x more deadly)
- "There will be no school next Thursday" (unexpected lead + concrete implication)
- Nordies gift-wrapping Macy's packages (unexpected service + concrete action)

### [MTS:X.3] The Emotional-Story Combination
**Core Principle:** Emotion makes people care; stories make people act. Together, they create the strongest possible call to action. The Truth antismoking campaign combined antiauthority emotion (anger at Big Tobacco) with a story (teens piling body bags outside tobacco headquarters). Floyd Lee combined transcendence emotion ("I am in charge of morale") with the story of Pegasus mess hall.

**Examples:**
- Rokia story ($2.38 donation) vs statistics ($1.14) vs both ($1.43 --- worse!)
- Challenge plots (Jared, Rose Blumkin) + perseverance emotion
- Connection plots (Good Samaritan) + empathy emotion
- Springboard stories (World Bank Zambia) + possibility emotion

### [MTS:X.4] The Simple-Credible Combination
**Core Principle:** A simple core message + a single powerful credential creates unshakeable conviction. The Sinatra Test is the purest expression: one example that proves everything. "We handled Harry Potter AND your brother's board exams" (Simple credential + Concrete trust).

**Examples:**
- Safexpress: Harry Potter delivery + board exam delivery = trustworthy for Bollywood films
- McDonough's textile factory: "38 safe chemicals out of 8,000" + "Swiss inspectors thought their instruments were broken"
- Reagan: "Are you better off than 4 years ago?" (simple question = testable credential)

## 3. Communication Frameworks

### [MTS:F.1] The Two-Step Process
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 1
**Framework:**
1. **Step 1: Find the core** --- identify the single most important truth (Commander's Intent)
2. **Step 2: Translate the core using SUCCESs** --- make it Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, and embed it in Stories

This two-step process IS the book's core message. Everything else is elaboration.

### [MTS:F.2] The Answer Stage vs. The Telling Others Stage
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Epilogue
**Framework:**
- **Answer Stage:** Use expertise to arrive at the idea. Requires deep knowledge, analysis, data.
- **Telling Others Stage:** Communicate the idea so it sticks. Requires SUCCESs principles.
- **The rub:** The same factors that help in the Answer Stage (expertise, data fluency, abstract thinking) BACKFIRE in the Telling Others Stage (Curse of Knowledge, data dumps, abstraction).
- **The gap:** "It's easy to graduate from medical school without ever taking a class in communication."

### [MTS:F.3] Springboard Communication Pattern
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 6 (Stephen Denning)
**Framework:**
1. Set up the problem briefly
2. Tell a concrete story that illustrates the desired future state
3. Let the audience generate their own implications
4. The story becomes "their idea"

**Why it works:** Traditional argumentation invites evaluation, debate, and counter-argument. Stories engage the "little voice inside the head" --- instead of arguing back, it starts building on the story. "Don't ignore the little voice. Instead, work in harmony with it."

### [MTS:F.4] The News-Teaser Approach
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Chapter 2
**Framework:**
Shift from "What information do I need to convey?" to "What questions do I want my audience to ask?" Use the structure of news teasers: hint at knowledge the audience is missing, creating a gap they want to fill.

**Application:**
- Instead of: "This year we targeted support from theatergoers under 35"
- Try: "This year we set out to answer a question: Why do people under 35, who make up 40% of our audience, provide only 10% of our donations?"

### [MTS:F.5] Fight Sticky with Stickier
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Epilogue --- "Unsticking an Idea"
**Framework:**
You can't unstick a sticky idea. There's no "Goo Gone for ideas." Instead:
1. **Fight with a stickier idea** --- redirect, don't erase
2. **Use sticky actions, not just messages** --- reliability races convinced people to sit on an explosion (automobile adoption)
3. **Redirect the emotional energy** --- WWII rumor control redirected anger AT rumormongers instead of arguing rumors were false

**Examples:**
- McDonald's earthworm rumor: Ray Kroc: "We couldn't afford to grind worms into meat. Hamburger costs $1.50/lb, night crawlers cost $6!"
- Goodtimes virus parody (fight bogus virus warnings with funnier parody that created a "schema of an overhyped warning")
- Automobile reliability races (17 years to establish public trust through demonstration, not argument)

## 4. Teaching Applications

### [MTS:T.1] Making Teaching Stick
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), "Teaching That Sticks" section
**Core Principle:** Teachers face the Curse of Knowledge daily --- every year, their understanding deepens, making it harder to imagine not knowing. The SUCCESs checklist serves as a "second language" --- Sticky vs Expertese. "Students don't speak Expertese. They do speak Sticky."

**Principle-by-Principle Teaching Applications:**

**Simple for Teaching:**
- Find the core of your lesson (Andrew Carl Singer: "What 3 things must a student know to get hired and make the university proud?")
- Anchor in existing knowledge (Bjorn Holdt: variables = cups of different types; coffee mugs store only true/false)

**Unexpected for Teaching:**
- Use curiosity gaps ("Did you know that 25,000 years ago a compass would point you toward the South Pole?")
- Create mysteries (San Diego Zoo: Which animal is stealing the petting zoo food? Students do DNA analysis to find out --- it was Ed the Pony)
- "Why Do Men Have Nipples?" --- ten seconds ago you weren't thinking about mammalian physiology

**Concrete for Teaching:**
- Functions taught with chirping crickets (temperature changes chirp rate --- students create the function themselves)
- Punctuation taught with macaroni shapes (elbow = comma/quotation marks, shell = period)
- Asian math teachers use "Computing in Context" 61% of lessons vs 31% in US

**Credible for Teaching:**
- "See for yourself" labs and experiments
- Amy Hyett: skeptical students spend 30 min alone in nature --- "almost every student has an illuminating experience"
- Statistics as relationships: "You're more likely to be struck by lightning than win the lottery"

**Emotional for Teaching:**
- Civil War: students saw bone as battlefield surgeons (15 min lesson remembered 10+ years later)
- Lab safety: dissolving cow eyeball in acid
- "Math is mental weight training" (higher Maslow: self-actualization, not base self-interest)

**Story for Teaching:**
- "Safe Night Out" entrepreneurial soap opera for accounting (students more likely to major in accounting, scored higher 2 years later)
- Greg Kim: story of friend's weight-loss inconsistency to reach unruly student John
- Professor Buckman's problem student story (simulation vs bullet-point tips)

### [MTS:T.2] Complete Teaching Example: Oceanography
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), "Teaching That Sticks" section
**Pattern:** Loudoun Academy of Science teachers used all six principles:
- **Simple:** Anchor in existing weather knowledge (NYC vs Madrid at same latitude)
- **Unexpected:** "Where will a bottle end up?" + 28,800 rubber duckies fell overboard and traveled the world
- **Concrete:** Message in a bottle, rubber duckies, hands-on water tanks with salinity/temperature
- **Credible:** "See for yourself" --- create your own ocean currents in a tank
- **Emotional:** Hope/mystery/anxiety of tossing a message into the sea
- **Story:** The tale of thousands of rubber duckies that fell overboard in 1992 and some arrived 12 years later

## 5. Talking Strategy

### [MTS:TS.1] Strategy Communication Framework
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), "Talking Strategy" section
**Core Principle:** External communications are usually far more sophisticated than internal. "Customer communication is taken very seriously, and employee communication isn't." A strategy is, at its core, a guide to behavior. If it doesn't manifest in action, it's inert --- "academic."

**Three Barriers to Talking Strategy:**
1. **Curse of Knowledge** --- leaders talk as if THEY were the audience ("unlock shareholder value")
2. **Decision Paralysis** --- strategy doesn't resolve trade-offs between competing goods
3. **Lack of Common Language** --- strategy is too abstract for two-way communication

**Solutions:**
1. **Be concrete** --- Trader Joe's "unemployed college professor who drives a very, very used Volvo" (target customer persona)
2. **Say something unexpected** --- if it's common sense, don't waste time communicating it; identify the uncommon sense
3. **Tell stories** --- "You can reconstruct the moral from the story, but you can't reconstruct the story from the moral"

### [MTS:TS.2] Strategy Stories
**Source:** Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), "Talking Strategy" section

**Costco "Salmon Stories":**
Jim Sinegal: Started selling salmon at $5.99/lb. Buyers got better product at better price → $5.29. Then pin bones and skin removed → $4.99. Then direct farm orders → $4.79. "We've used that story so much that other buyers come to me and say, 'I've got a salmon story to tell you.'" The moral ("relentless pursuit of ever-increasing quality at ever-decreasing prices") is implicit in the story but doesn't work well standalone.

**FedEx Purple Promise:**
Driver and ramp agent carried packages the last mile on foot after accident blocked road. Another driver persuaded a competitor's driver to take her on deliveries. These stories ARE the strategy --- "the most reliable shipping company in the world" --- in concrete, actionable form.

**Cranium CHIFF:**
"Clever, High-quality, Innovative, Friendly, Fun." Chinese manufacturer corrected the FOUNDER using CHIFF: "It's not CHIFF" (glued-together game piece). "There is no clearer proof that a strategy has been communicated properly than when a manufacturing supplier, in another country, with a different native language, uses it to correct the founder."

**Edison's "Muckers":**
"To muck in" = work together as mates. "To muck around" = fool around. Strategy statement masquerading as a nickname: given the trade-off between efficiency and experimentation, choose experimentation. "Muckers don't obsess over Gantt charts. Muckers muck."

**Savings & Loans Credit Union (Adelaide):**
"We don't want to be first, but we sure as hell don't want to be third." Fast-follower strategy. Competitor launched environmental credit card (flop); S&L copied idea with children's hospital card (hit). Front-liners can push back: "We don't want to be first. Why not wait?"

## 6. Sticky Ideas Checklist

### Pre-Flight Check
Before communicating any important idea, run through:

**1. CORE (Simple)**
- [ ] What is the single most important thing? (Commander's Intent)
- [ ] Can I express it in one sentence?
- [ ] Will someone who hears only this one sentence make correct decisions?
- [ ] Have I used schemas/analogies to leverage existing knowledge?

**2. ATTENTION (Unexpected)**
- [ ] What's counterintuitive about my message?
- [ ] Does it sound like common sense? (If yes, find the uncommon sense)
- [ ] Have I broken the audience's guessing machine on a dimension that matters?
- [ ] For long messages: Have I created curiosity gaps or a mystery structure?

**3. UNDERSTANDING (Concrete)**
- [ ] Can the audience see/hear/touch/taste/smell my idea?
- [ ] Have I translated abstractions into specific people doing specific things?
- [ ] Is there a shared "turf" where diverse people can collaborate?
- [ ] Have I maximized hooks (Velcro Theory)?

**4. BELIEF (Credible)**
- [ ] External: Do I have authorities or antiauthorities?
- [ ] Internal: Do I have vivid details, meaningful statistics, or Sinatra Test examples?
- [ ] Audience: Can they test the claim themselves (testable credentials)?
- [ ] Am I using statistics to illustrate relationships, not impress with numbers?

**5. CARING (Emotional)**
- [ ] Am I talking about an individual or a mass? (Mother Teresa principle)
- [ ] Am I triggering analytical thinking that would suppress emotion?
- [ ] Have I moved beyond Maslow's basement?
- [ ] Am I appealing to identity, not just self-interest?

**6. ACTION (Story)**
- [ ] Does my message provide simulation (how to act) or inspiration (energy to act)?
- [ ] Is it a Challenge, Connection, or Creativity plot --- and is that the right one for my goal?
- [ ] Could I use a springboard story to create buy-in?
- [ ] Am I spotting real stories rather than trying to create them from scratch?

**7. VILLAIN CHECK (Curse of Knowledge)**
- [ ] Would someone who doesn't know what I know understand this?
- [ ] Am I tapping or am I the listener?
- [ ] Have I tested this on an outsider?
- [ ] Am I using Expertese or Sticky?

## 7. Common Mistakes

### The Seven Deadly Sins of Communication

1. **Burying the Lead** --- Starting with interesting but irrelevant information instead of the core message. Fix: Forced prioritization. What would you telegraph if the line could be cut any second?

2. **Data Dumping** --- Sharing all the facts instead of the most meaningful relationship. Fix: Statistics as input, not output. Find the one comparison that illuminates. Nuclear warheads as BBs, not "5,000 nuclear warheads."

3. **Abstraction Addiction** --- Using "maximize shareholder value" when "THE low-fare airline" would actually guide behavior. Fix: Concrete language. "Cast members," not "employees."

4. **Gimmicky Surprise** --- Wolves attacking a marching band (memorable but worthless). Fix: Break guessing machines on a dimension that relates to the core message.

5. **Maslow's Basement** --- Assuming everyone is motivated only by money, security, and status. Fix: Try Aesthetic, Learning, Self-actualization, Transcendence. "I am in charge of morale, not food service."

6. **The Analytical Hat** --- Combining statistics with emotional appeals, reducing the emotional response. Fix: Lead with emotion, use statistics only to establish relationships. Never prime analytical thinking before asking for emotional engagement.

7. **The Edifice Trap** --- Believing your intellectual framework is more valuable than the stories that illustrate it. Fix: "You can extract a moral from a story, but you can't extract a story from a moral." The story IS the communication; the framework is for you.

## 8. The Stanford Speech Exercise

**Setup:** Students given crime data, asked to make 1-minute persuasive speeches. Rated by peers.

**Speaking Statistics:**
- Average speech: 2.5 statistics used
- Only 1 in 10 students tells a story
- Polished speakers get highest delivery ratings

**Remembering Statistics (10 minutes later):**
- 63% remember the stories
- Only 5% remember any individual statistic
- Almost no correlation between "speaking talent" and stickiness
- Foreign students (less polished English) are suddenly on par with native speakers

**Lesson:** "A community college student for whom English is a second language could easily outperform unwitting Stanford graduate students" --- if they used the SUCCESs principles. Charisma helps delivery, but it can't save a dense, unfocused message.
