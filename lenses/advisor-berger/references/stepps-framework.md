# STEPPS Framework — Complete Reference

> Source: Contagious: Why Things Catch On (Jonah Berger, 2013)
> Tag prefix: `[CTG`

## 1. [CTG:SC] Social Currency

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** People share things that make them look good to others. Just as we use money to buy products, we use social currency to achieve desired positive impressions among families, friends, and colleagues. What we talk about influences how others see us — knowing cool things makes people seem sharp and in the know.
**Mantra:** "We share things that make us look good."

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You need initial buzz and word-of-mouth ignition; your product/idea has something unusual, surprising, or exclusive about it; you want people to voluntarily talk about your brand
- AVOID WHEN: The "remarkable" element is disconnected from the product's core value proposition (see Trojan Horse problem in Stories); when overuse of exclusivity becomes snooty or alienating
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:E]` (remarkable things evoke surprise/awe), `[CTG:S]` (remarkable facts need narrative vessels), `[CTG:P]` (visible status symbols amplify social currency)

---

### [CTG:SC.1] Inner Remarkability

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** Remarkable things are unusual, extraordinary, or worthy of notice — and most importantly, worthy of remark. Finding the inner remarkability means identifying what makes something interesting, surprising, or novel. The key insight: remarkability can be found in ANY product by thinking about what makes it stand out.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your product seems boring or commodity-like; you need a conversation starter; you want media pickup
- AVOID WHEN: The remarkable element is disconnected from the product (GoldenPalace.com streaker problem — see `[CTG:S.2]`)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:E]` (surprise is high-arousal), `[CTG:PV]` (useful + remarkable = double driver)

**Key Examples:**
- **$100 cheesesteak (Barclay Prime):** Howard Wein broke the pattern of what a cheesesteak is — Kobe beef, lobster tail, champagne. Nobody talks about just another cheesesteak, but everyone talks about a $100 one. Media coverage from USA Today, Wall Street Journal, David Letterman.
- **Will It Blend? (Blendtec):** $50 marketing budget. Tom Dickson blended marbles, golf balls, iPhones. 300M+ views. Retail sales up 700%. A boring blender became the most remarkable kitchen appliance on the internet.
- **Snapple Facts:** "Real Fact #27: A ball of glass will bounce higher than a ball of rubber." Under-cap trivia that was so surprising people couldn't help but share. Became embedded in popular culture — websites chronicle facts, comedians reference them.
- **Black toilet paper:** Author put a roll of black toilet paper in his bathroom. It became the most talked-about topic at the party. Remarkability can be imposed on even the most mundane products.
- **The Blair Witch Project:** Mystery + controversy = remarkability. $35,000 budget → $248M worldwide. People debated whether it was real, which drove massive word-of-mouth.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ask: "What makes this product/idea interesting, surprising, or novel?"
2. Break a pattern people have come to expect (JetBlue vs. typical low-cost airlines — big seats, free DIRECTV)
3. Find extremes: Can the product do something no one thought possible?
4. Create mysteries or controversies that people need to discuss to resolve
5. Remember: Even mundane products have inner remarkability — you just have to find it

**Reversal:** Remarkability drives immediate word-of-mouth but does NOT sustain ongoing buzz on its own. A pirate costume is remarkable on Day 1 but not talked about 2 months later. Must combine with Triggers `[CTG:T]` for sustained conversation.

### [CTG:SC.2] Game Mechanics

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** Game mechanics — rules, feedback loops, levels, and leaderboards — keep people engaged, motivated, and wanting more. They motivate both internally (achieving milestones feels good) and interpersonally (social comparison — doing better than others). People don't just care about how they're doing; they care about performance relative to others.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You can quantify performance; you want ongoing engagement (not just one-time buzz); there's a competitive or status element possible
- AVOID WHEN: Metrics are confusing (too many badge types nobody understands); the gamification feels forced or patronizing
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:P]` (visible achievements — badges, levels — create public social proof), `[CTG:SC.3]` (exclusive rewards for top performers)

**Key Examples:**
- **Frequent flier programs (United Airlines):** 180M+ people accumulate miles. Less than 10% of miles are redeemed yearly, yet people take connecting flights just to hit status thresholds. The author paid MORE for a connecting flight to earn 222 extra miles for Premier Executive status.
- **Foursquare badges:** Check-in badges (Jetsetter, Mayor) provide visible status symbols. Users prominently display them on Facebook pages — spreading the Foursquare brand along the way.
- **Burberry Art of the Trench:** Users submit photos of themselves in Burberry trench coats. Selected photos appear on the website. The contest drove millions of views from 100+ countries and boosted sales 50%.
- **Harvard salary study:** Majority chose $50K salary (where peers earn $25K) over $100K salary (where peers earn $200K). People prefer to do better RELATIVELY, even if it means less in absolute terms.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. **Quantify performance:** Create metrics if none exist (miles flown, badges earned, check-ins made)
2. **Create discrete milestones:** People are more motivated as they get closer to achieving them (coffee punch cards — people buy more frequently near the 10th cup)
3. **Make achievements visible:** Badges, levels, and status symbols that can be shown to others
4. **Use intuitive systems:** Olympic medals (gold/silver/bronze) are instantly understood. Avoid confusing hierarchies (is diamond better than sapphire? — credit card problem)
5. **Enable publicizing:** Give people ways to share their achievements (post to Facebook, display on profile)

**Reversal:** Monetary incentives can crowd out intrinsic motivation `[INF:RC]`. Once you pay people to do something, they lose interest in doing it for free. Social incentives (social currency) are more effective long-term than financial ones.

### [CTG:SC.3] Making People Feel Like Insiders

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** Scarcity and exclusivity make products catch on by making them seem more desirable. If something is difficult to obtain, people assume it must be worth the effort. Having insider knowledge is social currency — telling others makes you look good. Two mechanisms: scarcity (limited availability due to demand/production/time) and exclusivity (accessible only to people meeting certain criteria).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You can genuinely limit availability (time, quantity, access); you want to create FOMO; you want members to become evangelists
- AVOID WHEN: Restrictions come across as snooty or standoffish (use "no, but..." instead of flat "no"); product needs mass adoption quickly
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC.1]` (insider access IS inherently remarkable), `[CTG:PV]` (exclusive deals = practical value + social currency)

**Key Examples:**
- **Please Don't Tell (secret bar):** No sign, no ads. Entrance through a phone booth in a hot dog restaurant. Reservations open at 3pm sharp, first-come-first-served. Fully booked by 3:30. "The most powerful marketing is personal recommendation."
- **Rue La La:** Same products as struggling SmartBargains.com, but invitation-only + 24-hour flash sales. 40-50% of items sell out in the first hour. Sold for $350M. The mere fact of limited access transformed perception.
- **McDonald's McRib:** National launch failed. But making it available only at certain locations at certain times created Facebook groups ("bring back the McRib!"), a McRib locator website, and obsessive fans. A mix of tripe, heart, and stomach meat became a cultural phenomenon.
- **Disney Vault:** Taking beloved movies OFF the market increases perceived value and creates urgency to buy.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. **Limit time:** Flash sales, seasonal availability, "today only" — Rue La La model
2. **Limit quantity:** "Only X remaining" or "limit N per customer" — quantity limits increase sales 50%+
3. **Limit access:** Invitation-only, members-only, by-application — Rue La La, Please Don't Tell
4. **Start restrictive, then relax:** Build demand with exclusivity, then gradually open access (Rue La La eventually allowed email signup)
5. **Give people the tools to share:** Please Don't Tell hands you a business card with the phone number at the end of your visit — they WANT you to share the secret
6. **Handle rejection gracefully:** "No, but..." preserves allure while maintaining satisfaction

**Reversal:** Making access difficult is different from making it impossible. If people hear "no" too much, they go elsewhere. The balance is key.

## 2. [CTG:T] Triggers

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Triggers are stimuli in the environment that prompt people to think about related things. Top of mind leads to tip of tongue. While Social Currency gets people talking, Triggers KEEP them talking. Interesting products get more immediate word-of-mouth, but triggered products get more ongoing word-of-mouth. The most underrated principle: frequency of triggering matters more than how interesting something is.
**Mantra:** "Top of mind, tip of tongue."

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You need sustained word-of-mouth over time (not just initial buzz); your behavior change requires ongoing reminders; your product is consumed or needed repeatedly
- AVOID WHEN: You only need a one-time viral spike; the trigger would be forced or unnatural
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC]` (triggers amplify whatever social currency exists), `[CTG:PV]` (triggered practical value = shared when relevant), `[CTG:P]` (visible cues in environment serve as triggers)

**Critical Insight (Berger's data):** Honey Nut Cheerios gets more word-of-mouth than Disney World. Disney World is more interesting but less frequently triggered. Cheerios mentions on Twitter spike every day at 7:30-8:00am (breakfast time) and shift later on weekends.

### [CTG:T.1] Frequency of Trigger

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** How often a stimulus occurs is a key factor in trigger effectiveness. Coffee is a better trigger than hot chocolate because people encounter it year-round (hot chocolate is seasonal). Weekends are a better trigger than holidays (Michelob switched from "Holidays are made for Michelob" to "Weekends are made for Michelob" — much more successful).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Choosing what to link your product to; designing a campaign tagline; deciding where to place messaging
- AVOID WHEN: The frequent trigger is already associated with too many things (dilution — see `[CTG:T.3]`)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:T.2]` (frequent AND habitat-appropriate = maximum trigger power)

**Key Examples:**
- **Kit Kat + Coffee:** Colleen Chorak revived the declining Kit Kat brand by pairing it with coffee — "a break's best friend." Coffee is consumed multiple times daily, year-round. Sales grew from $300M to $500M. "Kit Kat and cantaloupe" is equally alliterative but cantaloupe is rare.
- **Michelob:** "Holidays are made for Michelob" → "Weekends are made for Michelob." Holidays happen rarely; weekends happen every week.
- **Rebecca Black's "Friday":** The song was equally bad every day, but YouTube searches spiked every Friday. The day of the week acted as a weekly trigger that contributed to 300M+ views.
- **Mars bar sales (1997):** Mars bars (named after founder Franklin Mars, not the planet) saw an unexpected sales uptick when NASA's Pathfinder mission landed on Mars. The media coverage of planet Mars triggered thoughts of the candy.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Map your product's natural triggers — what environmental cues already remind people of it?
2. Evaluate trigger frequency: daily > weekly > monthly > seasonal > rare
3. Choose high-frequency anchors: meals, commutes, morning routines, common greetings
4. Test: Would this trigger remind someone of my product when they're far from a purchase moment?

**Reversal:** Frequency alone isn't enough — must be balanced with link strength (see `[CTG:T.3]`).

### [CTG:T.2] Growing the Habitat

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Products and ideas have habitats — sets of triggers that cause people to think about them. Hot dogs have a large habitat (barbecues, baseball, summer, dachshunds). Ethiopian food has a small habitat. You can grow an idea's habitat by creating new links to stimuli in the environment. Kit Kat wasn't naturally associated with coffee, but repeated pairing created the link.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your product's natural habitat is small; you want to expand usage occasions; you're entering a new market
- AVOID WHEN: You're already well-triggered in your primary context (risk of diluting the association)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:T.1]` (grow toward frequent triggers), `[CTG:T.4]` (competitors' triggers can become yours)

**Key Examples:**
- **Kit Kat + Coffee:** Created an entirely new trigger link. Radio spots repeatedly paired the two. Kit Kat had no natural connection to coffee before the campaign.
- **Boston Market + Dinner:** Boston Market was seen as a lunch place. Campaign associating it with dinner ("Thinking about dinner? Think about Boston Market!") increased word-of-mouth by 20% among people who previously only thought of it as lunch.
- **Dining hall trays + fruits/vegetables:** "Each and every dining-hall tray needs five fruits and veggies a day." Students hated the slogan (called it "corny") but it increased fruit/vegetable consumption 25% because trays in the dining hall triggered the message at exactly the right time.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. List your product's current habitat (all existing triggers)
2. Identify high-frequency stimuli in your target audience's environment
3. Create pairings through repeated association (advertising, placement, partnerships)
4. Ensure the trigger occurs NEAR the desired behavior (see `[CTG:T.5]`)
5. Test the new link: Does exposure to the trigger now bring your product to mind?

**Reversal:** Growing the habitat takes repetition. A single ad won't create a lasting trigger link.

### [CTG:T.3] Link Strength

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** The more things a given cue is associated with, the weaker any given association. Like poking holes in a paper cup — one hole gives a strong stream; many holes give weak trickles. The color red is associated with roses, love, Coca-Cola, fast cars — so it's not a particularly strong trigger for any of them. "Peanut butter" strongly triggers "jelly" because the link is unique and strong.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Choosing between potential triggers; evaluating whether your current trigger is effective
- AVOID WHEN: N/A — this is a diagnostic principle
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:T.1]` (balance frequency with strength — frequent + unique = ideal)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Prefer fresher, more original links over ubiquitous ones
2. Test: Say the trigger word to 10 people. How many immediately think of your product?
3. If your trigger is overloaded (associated with many things), find a more specific one
4. Own a narrow trigger strongly rather than a broad trigger weakly

**Reversal:** Extremely unique triggers may be too rare to be useful. Balance uniqueness with frequency.

### [CTG:T.4] Poison Parasite Strategy

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Competitors can be used as triggers. By making a rival's message trigger your own, you inject "poison" into their communication. This is especially powerful for underfunded organizations competing against well-resourced rivals.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're outspent by competitors; the competitor's messaging is pervasive; you need to piggyback on existing awareness
- AVOID WHEN: Your competitor is not well-known enough to serve as a trigger
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC.1]` (subverting a famous brand is inherently remarkable)

**Key Examples:**
- **Anti-smoking vs. Marlboro:** An antismoking campaign spoofed Marlboro's iconic cowboy ads with "Bob, I've got emphysema." Now every Marlboro ad triggered the antismoking message.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify your strongest competitor's most visible messaging
2. Create content that links their message to your counter-message
3. Ensure the association is strong enough that seeing the original triggers the parody
4. This works best as a judo move — using the opponent's own strength against them

### [CTG:T.5] Context and Timing

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Triggers must occur near where the desired behavior takes place. A bath mat PSA is memorable but useless when you can't buy a bath mat in your bathroom. The trigger reminds you at the wrong time. Effective triggers prompt action when action is possible.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Designing where/when to place messages; choosing trigger linkages; debugging why a campaign isn't converting
- AVOID WHEN: N/A — always consider context
- COMBINES WITH: All other trigger sub-principles

**Key Examples:**
- **Bath mat PSA (New Zealand):** Brilliant, memorable ad showing a man slipping in the shower. Problem: you can't buy a bath mat in a bathroom. The trigger is in the wrong location.
- **NYC DOH anti-soda campaign:** Fat pours out of a soda can. The trigger (seeing a soda can) occurs exactly when you might drink soda — perfect timing.
- **Reusable grocery bags:** Trigger at the grocery store (too late — bags are at home). Need trigger when leaving the house (shopping list = good trigger).
- **Safe-sex messages on campus:** Posted at health center, far from frat houses where students actually drink. Message is encountered in the wrong context.
- **Halloween + orange products:** People thought more about orange soda and Reese's Pieces the day before Halloween (orange pumpkins everywhere) than a week later. Triggers are seasonal/contextual.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Map the user's journey: WHERE are they when they need to act?
2. Place triggers at or near the point of action
3. Time triggers to when the behavior is possible (not just when attention is high)
4. For delayed-action products, build trigger chains (trigger at home → trigger at store)

### [CTG:T.6] Negative Publicity and Triggers

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Even negative word-of-mouth can increase sales if it informs or reminds people that a product exists. Triggers explain this paradox: being talked about (even negatively) keeps you top of mind.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Assessing damage from bad reviews; deciding whether to respond to criticism; evaluating negative press for unknown products
- AVOID WHEN: The product is already well-known (negative press hurts established brands)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:E]` (anger is high-arousal → drives sharing)

**Key Examples:**
- **Unknown authors + bad reviews:** Negative NYT reviews increased sales of books by unknown authors by 45%. The review informed people the book existed.
- **$60 Tuscan wine + "stinky socks":** A prominent wine website called it "redolent of stinky socks." Sales rose 5%.
- **Shake Weight:** Widely ridiculed by media and consumers. Did $50M in sales.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. For unknown products: any attention (even negative) can increase awareness
2. For established products: negative publicity hurts — protect the brand
3. The determining factor is whether people already know the product exists

## 3. [CTG:E] Emotion

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** When we care, we share. Emotional things get shared more. But the KEY insight is NOT simply that positive emotions help and negative emotions hurt. The real distinction is between HIGH-AROUSAL emotions (which drive sharing) and LOW-AROUSAL emotions (which suppress it). Physiological arousal — the activation and readiness for action — is the mechanism.
**Mantra:** "When we care, we share."

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You need content to go viral; you want to drive sharing of a specific piece; you need to kindle the fire of word-of-mouth
- AVOID WHEN: Your content naturally triggers low-arousal emotions (sadness, contentment) — must reframe toward high-arousal
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC]` (awe-inspiring things provide social currency), `[CTG:S]` (emotional stories are the best Trojan Horses), `[CTG:PV]` (useful + emotional = top virality)

---

### [CTG:E.1] The Arousal Model (High vs. Low)

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Emotions have two dimensions: valence (positive/negative) and arousal (high/low activation). High-arousal emotions drive action and sharing; low-arousal emotions suppress them. This is the CENTRAL finding from Berger's NYT Most E-Mailed analysis.

**The Emotion Matrix:**

| | HIGH AROUSAL (drives sharing) | LOW AROUSAL (suppresses sharing) |
|---|---|---|
| **POSITIVE** | Awe, Excitement, Amusement (Humor) | Contentment |
| **NEGATIVE** | Anger, Anxiety | Sadness |

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Choosing which emotion to evoke in content; evaluating why content isn't getting shared; reframing messaging
- AVOID WHEN: N/A — this is the core diagnostic tool for emotion-driven sharing

**Key Data (from NYT Most E-Mailed analysis):**
- Positive articles were 13% more likely to make the Most E-Mailed list
- Awe-inspiring articles were 30% more likely to make the list
- Articles evoking anger or anxiety were MORE likely to make the list (despite being negative)
- Articles evoking sadness were 16% LESS likely to make the list
- Interesting articles were 25% more likely; useful articles were 30% more likely

**Actionable Framework:**
1. **Default to high-arousal positive:** Awe and excitement are safest drivers
2. **Anger > sadness for negative content:** Make people mad, not sad. Get them fired up about polar bears, not just bummed out
3. **Humor works because it's high-arousal:** Not because it's funny per se, but because amusement activates
4. **Contentment is a trap:** Feeling content is pleasant but doesn't drive sharing. A relaxing massage doesn't make you want to tell everyone about it
5. **Physical arousal spills over:** Exercise increases sharing — people who jogged in place for 60 seconds were TWICE as likely to share an article afterward. Implications for gym ads, exciting TV show ad breaks, etc.

**Reversal:** Content can be emotionally arousing but still fail if the brand isn't woven into the narrative (see `[CTG:S.2]` Valuable Virality).

### [CTG:E.2] Awe

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Awe is the sense of wonder and amazement that occurs when someone is inspired by great knowledge, beauty, sublimity, or might. It's the experience of confronting something greater than yourself. Awe expands one's frame of reference and drives self-transcendence. Among all emotions, awe was the strongest predictor of NYT Most E-Mailed sharing.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have something genuinely impressive, beautiful, or mind-expanding to show; your product represents a breakthrough or unexpected capability
- AVOID WHEN: The awe-inspiring element is disconnected from your brand (Evian Roller Babies problem)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC.1]` (awe-inspiring things are inherently remarkable), `[CTG:S]` (awe-inspiring narratives get retold)

**Key Examples:**
- **"The Mysterious Cough, Caught on Film" (NYT):** Denise Grady's article about schlieren photography of a cough went viral. Low social currency, low practical value — but the image of a cough visualized in air was awe-inspiring.
- **Susan Boyle on Britain's Got Talent:** 100M+ views in 9 days. The contrast between her appearance and her voice created overwhelming awe. "So powerful, so beautiful that it makes the hair on the back of your neck stand up."
- **Science articles on NYT:** Consistently more shared than politics, fashion, or business — because they chronicle discoveries that evoke awe.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ask: "Will this make people feel wonder, amazement, or inspiration?"
2. Show something greater than the individual (natural beauty, human achievement, scientific breakthrough)
3. Create contrast or surprise (Susan Boyle's gap between appearance and voice)
4. Use the "Three Whys" technique: ask "Why is this important?" three times to drill down to the emotional core

---

### [CTG:E.3] Anger and Anxiety (Productive Negative Emotions)

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Anger and anxiety are high-arousal negative emotions that INCREASE sharing. When we're angry, we yell at customer service reps and write furious tweets. When we're anxious, we check and recheck things. Both states drive action — including the action of sharing.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Using negative information strategically (public health, social justice, competitive attack); your product/service resolves anger or anxiety; monitoring brand crises
- AVOID WHEN: Anger might backfire onto your brand; anxiety creates paralysis rather than action
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:PV]` (anger at a problem + practical solution = powerful combination), `[CTG:S]` (angry customer stories spread fast)

**Key Examples:**
- **"United Breaks Guitars" (Dave Carroll):** Furious musician wrote a song about United Airlines breaking his guitar and stonewalling his complaint. 3M+ views in 10 days. United's stock dropped 10% ($180M). Anger is rocket fuel for sharing.
- **Motrin Babywearing ad:** Mommy bloggers were angered by the implication that babywearing was a fashion statement. Anger snowballed → boycott → NYT coverage → Motrin pulled the ad.
- **Wall Street bonuses during economic downturn:** NYT articles about this topic evoked anger and were highly shared.
- **BMW's "The Hire" (2001):** Short films with kidnappings, FBI raids, near-death experiences. Fear and anxiety (high-arousal) drove 11M views in 4 months. BMW sales up 12%.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. For campaigns: Make people angry about a problem, then present your solution
2. For crisis management: Monitor for high-arousal negative words ("pissed off," "angry," "furious") — these signals predict snowballing
3. For negative press defense: Address high-arousal complaints immediately. Sadness fizzles; anger amplifies
4. Disgust is also high-arousal: NYC DOH "Man Drinks Fat" PSA — blobs of fat pouring from a soda can. Gross but shared.

**Reversal:** Uncontrolled anger directed at your brand can be devastating (United Airlines lost $180M). Monitor and respond quickly to angry chatter.

### [CTG:E.4] Focus on Feelings, Not Features

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Marketing messages tend to focus on information. But information alone rarely changes behavior. Teens don't smoke because they think it's healthy. People who eat Big Macs aren't oblivious to health risks. We need to focus on feelings — the underlying emotions that motivate action.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your messaging is feature-heavy and not getting shared; you need to humanize a technical product; you want to move people beyond information to action
- AVOID WHEN: The audience specifically needs technical specs (B2B procurement, medical professionals)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:S]` (stories are the primary vessel for conveying feelings), `[CTG:PV]` (feelings + usefulness = sharing)

**Key Examples:**
- **Google's "Parisian Love":** Online search — one of the least emotional products imaginable. The Creative Lab told a love story entirely through Google search queries: "study abroad Paris France" → "translate tu es très mignon" → "how to assemble a crib." No people, no voices — just searches. Emotional knockout. "The best results don't show up in a search engine, they show up in people's lives."
- **The "Three Whys" technique:** Why is search important? → People want information quickly → So they can get answers → So they can connect with people, achieve their goals, and fulfill their dreams. Now THAT's emotional.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Apply "Three Whys": Write down why people use your product. Ask "Why is this important?" three times. Each level gets more emotional.
2. Tell human stories, not feature lists
3. Show consequences and outcomes, not specifications
4. "People don't want to feel like they're being told something — they want to be entertained, they want to be moved." — Anthony Cafaro, Google Creative Lab

### [CTG:E.5] Arousal Spillover

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** ANY physiological arousal increases sharing — not just emotional arousal. Exercise, turbulent plane rides, exciting TV shows — all increase the tendency to share whatever content comes next. Arousal from one source spills over to boost transmission of unrelated content.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Choosing ad placement; timing content distribution; designing physical spaces for word-of-mouth
- AVOID WHEN: N/A — this is a placement/timing optimization
- COMBINES WITH: All other STEPPS principles (arousal amplifies everything)

**Key Examples:**
- **Exercise experiment:** Students who jogged in place for 60 seconds were 75% likely to share an article vs. 33% in the relaxed group — more than TWICE as likely. The article was the same; only the arousal differed.
- **TV show ad placement:** Ads during exciting game shows (Deal or No Deal) or anxiety-inducing crime dramas (CSI) should generate more sharing than ads during documentaries.
- **Gym advertising:** People are already physiologically aroused at the gym. Any content they encounter should be more likely to be shared.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Place content where people are already aroused (gyms, sports events, exciting shows)
2. Time ads near peak-arousal moments within content (crime show peak tension, game show final reveal)
3. Design experiences that generate arousal BEFORE delivering your message
4. Caution: Arousal causes oversharing — people disclose more than intended after treadmill, car accident, turbulent flight

## 4. [CTG:P] Public

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** If something is built to show, it's built to grow. People imitate what they can see — "Monkey see, monkey do." Making things more observable makes them easier to imitate, which makes them more likely to become popular. Observability has a huge impact on whether products catch on. The key insight: behavior is public and thoughts are private, which creates systematic distortions in what people think others believe.
**Mantra:** "Built to show, built to grow."

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You want social proof to drive adoption; your product's usage is currently invisible; you need to make private support visible
- AVOID WHEN: Making something public could highlight undesired behaviors (anti-drug ads paradox — see `[CTG:P.4]`)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC]` (visible achievements = social currency), `[CTG:T]` (visible products serve as environmental triggers), `[CTG:E]` (seeing others' enthusiasm amplifies emotional arousal)

---

### [CTG:P.1] Observability and Social Proof

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** People imitate because others' choices provide information ("social proof"). If lots of people eat at a restaurant, it's probably good. If a kidney has been refused by 99 people, it must be bad (even if those refusals were for unrelated reasons). Observability determines HOW STRONG social proof effects are — in cities where driving is more visible (LA vs. NYC), social influence on car purchases is stronger.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: People are uncertain about your product; you have existing happy customers whose behavior isn't visible; you need to overcome inertia
- AVOID WHEN: Social proof would highlight low adoption (empty restaurant problem)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:P.2]` (self-advertising products maximize observability), `[CTG:P.3]` (behavioral residue extends observability beyond usage)

**Key Examples:**
- **Apple logo flip:** Steve Jobs realized that having the logo face the USER when closed meant it was upside-down to EVERYONE ELSE when open. They flipped it — observability trumped user orientation.
- **Car purchases (1.5M sales study):** 1 in 8 cars sold was influenced by social proof. Effect was stronger in LA (car commutes = more visible) than NYC (subway commutes = less visible), and in Miami (sunny = visible) vs. Seattle (rainy = less visible).
- **Kidney donation refusal:** 97.1% of kidney offers are refused. MIT researcher found that social proof (99 people turned it down before you → must be bad) leads 1 in 10 people to refuse kidneys they SHOULD have accepted.
- **MBA career convergence:** Diverse career interests at program start → 66%+ want banking/consulting by end. Social proof snowballs: 20% becomes 30% becomes majority.

---

### [CTG:P.2] Products That Advertise Themselves

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** Design products that create observable signals of usage. Every time someone uses the product, it should transmit social proof to potential adopters. This is like "advertising without an advertising budget."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have limited marketing budget; product usage naturally involves others seeing it; you're in early adoption phase
- AVOID WHEN: Users might be embarrassed by visible branding; the context is private
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC.2]` (visible branding can signal status), `[CTG:P.3]` (add behavioral residue for when product isn't in use)

**Key Examples:**
- **Hotmail:** Every email sent from Hotmail included "Get Your Private, Free E-mail from Hotmail." 8.5M subscribers in just over a year. Bought by Microsoft for $400M.
- **"Sent from my iPhone":** Default email signature that most users don't change. Provides social currency AND observable social proof.
- **Apple's white headphones (iPod):** Most competing devices had black headphones. White stood out visually, making it easy to see how many people were switching from Walkman to iPod.
- **Louboutin red soles:** Christian Louboutin added red-lacquered soles to make shoes instantly recognizable, even for people who know nothing about the brand.
- **Distinctive shapes/sounds:** Pringles tube, Microsoft boot sound, all serve as product identifiers.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Add visible branding or distinctive design that's seen during product use
2. Default signatures/watermarks that users optionally keep (but most don't change)
3. Create distinctive visual or audio identity (shape, color, sound)
4. Consider: "When someone uses my product, can a passerby tell they're using it?"

---

### [CTG:P.3] Behavioral Residue

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** Behavioral residue is the physical traces or remnants that actions leave behind. While products advertise themselves during use, behavioral residue sticks around even AFTER use. Wristbands, stickers, bags, t-shirts — all provide ongoing observable signals.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Product usage is brief or infrequent; you need ongoing social proof between purchases; support for your cause is invisible
- AVOID WHEN: The residue would be used privately (makeup bags in bathrooms = low observability)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC]` (people keep/display residue that provides social currency), `[CTG:P.1]` (residue provides social proof long after the action)

**Key Examples:**
- **Livestrong wristbands:** Made yellow (striking, gender-neutral, rarely seen color). 85M+ sold. "The nice thing about a wristband is that it lives on. The bike ride doesn't." — Scott MacEachern, Nike. Even Armstrong's agent called it "a stupid idea" initially.
- **"I Voted" stickers:** Made the private act of voting publicly observable even after leaving the polling station. Reminds others to vote and provides social proof.
- **Lululemon bags:** Made of sturdy plastic (hard to throw away). People reuse them for groceries and errands, creating ongoing brand exposure.
- **Victoria's Secret / Tiffany bags:** People reuse them as gym bags, lunch bags — creating behavioral residue for status brands.
- **Facebook "Like" buttons:** ABC News saw 250% increase in Facebook traffic after installing them. Each "Like" is behavioral residue visible to friends.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ask: "What physical trace does using my product leave behind?"
2. Make residue visible AND durable (wristband > sticker > receipt)
3. Make residue desirable to keep/display (social currency component)
4. Choose publicly visible residue over privately used (gym bag > makeup case)
5. Digital residue counts: reviews, likes, shares, badges on profiles

---

### [CTG:P.4] Making the Private Public (and When NOT To)

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** Many behaviors, opinions, and preferences are private — invisible to others. Making the private public (Movember, "I Voted" stickers) can drive adoption. But WARNING: making the public private is sometimes necessary. Anti-drug campaigns that show "lots of kids use drugs" INCREASE drug use by providing social proof. Publicizing bad behavior can normalize it.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You want to increase a positive behavior that's currently invisible; you need to correct false norms (students think others drink more than they actually do)
- AVOID WHEN: You want to DECREASE a behavior — never publicize how many people do the wrong thing
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:E.3]` (anger at hidden problems can be channeled when made public)

**Key Examples:**
- **Movember (moustaches for men's cancer):** Started with 30 friends in Melbourne. Grew to $174M+ raised worldwide. Moustaches made invisible cancer support visible. "Walking, talking billboards for the cause."
- **Anti-drug PSAs INCREASED drug use:** "Just Say No" and $1B National Youth Anti-Drug Media Campaign. Kids who saw the ads were MORE likely to smoke marijuana. The ads signaled that lots of other kids were doing drugs — social proof.
- **Binge drinking at Arizona:** Koreen Johannessen found most students were uncomfortable with binge drinking. But "behavior is public and thoughts are private" — they saw others drinking, assumed they were the outlier, and drank more. Solution: ads stating the TRUE norm ("69% have four or fewer drinks"). Heavy drinking decreased 30%.
- **Music piracy messaging:** "Only 37% of music was paid for" and "30 billion songs illegally downloaded" — inadvertently provides social proof that piracy is normal.
- **Petrified Forest theft:** Sign saying "many past visitors have removed wood" DOUBLED theft. Sign asking people to "preserve the natural state" reduced it.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. To INCREASE behavior: make existing good behavior visible (voting stickers, moustaches)
2. To DECREASE behavior: highlight the positive norm, NOT the prevalence of bad behavior
3. If most people are doing the right thing, publicize THAT (Johannessen's approach)
4. Never use phrases like "X million people are doing [bad thing]" — it normalizes
5. Frame as "join the majority who [good thing]" rather than "don't be like the minority who [bad thing]"

## 5. [CTG:PV] Practical Value

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** People share practically valuable information to help others. It's like a modern-day barn raising. Sharing useful things strengthens social bonds, helps friends and family, and feels good. If Social Currency is about the sender (how sharing makes them look), Practical Value is about the receiver (how sharing helps them). Berger's easiest principle to apply — almost every product has something useful about it.
**Mantra:** "News you can use."

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your product saves time, money, or improves life quality; you have expert knowledge to package; you want to reach older demographics (who are prolific sharers of practical content)
- AVOID WHEN: The practical value is buried in too much information; the deal doesn't seem remarkable enough to cut through clutter
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC]` (remarkable deals provide social currency), `[CTG:E]` (useful + emotional = most shared NYT articles), `[CTG:S]` (practical info embedded in stories travels farther)

---

### [CTG:PV.1] The Psychology of Deals (Prospect Theory)

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** People evaluate deals relative to reference points, not absolute values. A grill discounted from $350 to $250 seems better than the same grill priced at $240 (down from $255), even though $240 < $250. Based on Kahneman & Tversky's Nobel Prize-winning prospect theory. Reference points, diminishing sensitivity, and framing all affect whether a deal seems shareable.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Setting prices, framing discounts, designing promotional offers
- AVOID WHEN: Your audience is highly analytical and will see through inflated reference points
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:PV.2]` (Rule of 100 for optimal framing), `[CTG:SC.3]` (exclusive deals = social currency + practical value)

**Key Examples:**
- **BBQ grill experiment:** 75% would buy at $250 (marked down from $350). Only 22% would buy at $240 (marked down from $255). Same grill, HIGHER price in scenario A — but it seems like a better deal because of the reference point.
- **Infomercials:** "You might expect to pay $100 or even $200... but right now, only $39.99!" Sets high reference point to make actual price seem like a steal.
- **"On sale" label effect:** Anderson & Simester study: merely labeling items as "on sale" (without changing the price) increased demand by 50%+. The word "sale" itself shifts perception.
- **Clock radio vs. TV ($10 saving):** 83% would drive 20 min to save $10 on a $35 clock radio. Only 13% would drive to save $10 on a $650 TV. Same $10 saving — but diminishing sensitivity makes it feel smaller relative to the higher reference.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Set clear reference points: show "original price" / "was" price
2. Use "sale" labels (even the word alone lifts demand)
3. Understand diminishing sensitivity: same dollar discount means less on expensive items
4. Avoid perpetual sales: if everything is always on sale, people adjust reference points downward

---

### [CTG:PV.2] The Rule of 100

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** A simple heuristic for choosing between percentage vs. dollar framing for discounts. It maximizes the perceived value of any given discount.

**The Rule:**
- If product price is **UNDER $100** → use **percentage discount** (20% off sounds bigger than $5 off a $25 shirt)
- If product price is **OVER $100** → use **dollar discount** ($200 off sounds bigger than 10% off a $2,000 laptop)

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Framing any promotional offer, sale, or discount
- AVOID WHEN: N/A — always applies to discount framing
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:PV.1]` (Rule of 100 is a sub-principle of prospect theory framing)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Check: Is the price above or below $100?
2. Below $100: frame as percentage off
3. Above $100: frame as dollar amount off
4. The goal is always to make the number LOOK as large as possible

---

### [CTG:PV.3] Highlighting Incredible Value

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Deals need to cut through the clutter to get shared. We're bombarded with deals constantly — a deal has to seem truly incredible to generate word-of-mouth. Surprising deals that exceed expectations get shared more. Counter-intuitively, making promotions MORE RESTRICTIVE can make them more effective.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your deal is good but not getting shared; you want to increase the perceived value without changing the actual discount
- AVOID WHEN: Over-restriction alienates customers
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:SC.3]` (restrictions create insider feeling), `[CTG:PV.1]` (reference points set expectations to be exceeded)

**Key Examples:**
- **Quantity limits:** "Limit 3 per customer" increases sales by 50%+. People assume: "if there's a limit, the deal must be REALLY good."
- **Time limits:** "Available today only" creates urgency and signals exceptional value.
- **Exclusive access:** Hotel "exclusive rates" for loyalty members, restaurant "soft openings" — restricted availability makes deals seem better.
- **Perpetual sales problem:** Rug stores always offering 70% off → people stop seeing it as a deal. Too many "sale" items in a store actually REDUCE purchase.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Make deals surprising — exceed what people expect to get
2. Use time restrictions: limited-time offers signal genuine value
3. Use quantity restrictions: purchase limits signal high demand
4. Use access restrictions: member-only / invitation-only deals
5. Don't overdo sales: if everything is always discounted, nothing feels special
6. Make value visible: shopper discount cards hide savings on receipts — ring a bell, post a sign

---

### [CTG:PV.4] Packaging Knowledge and Expertise

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Useful information is another form of practical value. People share advice, tips, how-tos. The key is PACKAGING: short, focused, easy-to-parse formats spread better than dense information dumps. Narrower content may actually be MORE viral than broad content because it reminds people of a specific friend.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have domain expertise; you want content marketing that drives word-of-mouth; you want to build brand authority
- AVOID WHEN: Your expertise can't be distilled into actionable nuggets
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:S]` (embed expertise in narratives), `[CTG:E]` (expertise that evokes awe or surprise spreads faster)

**Key Examples:**
- **Ken Craig's corn video (86-year-old):** One simple trick for shucking corn without silk. 5M+ views. Topped the charts for people 55+. Pure practical value.
- **Vanguard MoneyWhys newsletter:** Short monthly emails with financial tips. People forward them to friends who need specific advice — and spread the Vanguard brand along the way.
- **NYT Most E-Mailed:** Health and education articles were among the most shared — because they provide practical value (live longer, educate kids better).
- **Vacuum cleaner hikers:** People hiking in the mountains were talking about vacuum cleaners. Not triggered, not emotional, not remarkable — just pure practical value exchange.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. **Short, focused formats:** Lists ("5 ways to..."), tips, one key insight per piece (not 25 links)
2. **Narrow beats broad:** An Ethiopian food review reaches fewer people but reminds you of that ONE friend who loves Ethiopian food — compelling you to share
3. **Package for forwarding:** Make it easy to see the main points at a glance
4. **Demonstrate expertise through helping:** Vanguard teaches financial literacy AND builds brand authority simultaneously

### [CTG:PV.5] Truth and Misinformation

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** The drive to share helpful information is so powerful it can make false ideas succeed. The vaccines-cause-autism myth spread because it was practically valuable (protect your kids!) even though it was false. People weren't trying to spread misinformation — they genuinely wanted to help.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Evaluating whether information should be shared; creating content that competes with misinformation
- AVOID WHEN: N/A — always verify before sharing
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:E]` (fear/anxiety about kids' health amplified the vaccine myth)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Verify information before sharing — especially health/safety claims
2. For counter-messaging: practical value can be used to spread TRUE information too
3. Speed matters: correct misinformation quickly, before practical value drives false sharing

## 6. [CTG:S] Stories

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** People don't think in terms of information — they think in terms of narratives. Stories are the original form of entertainment and the primary way cultural learning happens. Stories act as VESSELS that carry information under the guise of idle chatter. But the critical challenge is making virality VALUABLE — ensuring the brand is integral to the story, not an afterthought.
**Mantra:** "Information travels under the guise of idle chatter."

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You need to package information for natural sharing; your brand message is too dry/commercial for direct sharing; you want word-of-mouth that actually benefits your brand
- AVOID WHEN: The story is disconnected from the brand (GoldenPalace.com problem)
- COMBINES WITH: ALL other STEPPS principles — stories are the delivery mechanism for Social Currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public observability, and Practical Value

---

### [CTG:S.1] Stories as Trojan Horses

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Stories carry lessons, morals, information, and brand messages just as the Trojan Horse carried Greek soldiers. People don't like seeming like walking advertisements, but they naturally share stories. The information comes along for the ride. A good narrative provides "psychological cover" — people share it because it's interesting, but the brand benefits embedded within.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your core value proposition is hard to share directly; you need word-of-mouth that doesn't feel like marketing; you want lasting brand associations
- AVOID WHEN: You can communicate value directly (sometimes a clear product demo is better than a story)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:E]` (emotional stories are the most powerful Trojan Horses), `[CTG:SC]` (remarkable stories confer social currency on the teller)

**Key Examples:**
- **Lands' End coat replacement:** Cousin buys coat, zipper breaks, calls customer service expecting the worst. They send a free replacement via 2-day mail. The STORY is about amazing customer service. But embedded in it: (1) topcoats aren't warm enough for real winters, (2) down coats work, (3) Lands' End makes warm coats, (4) Lands' End has outstanding service, (5) if something breaks, they fix it.
- **Jared Fogle (Subway):** Lost 245 pounds eating Subway sandwiches. The STORY is about incredible weight loss. But embedded: (1) Subway has healthy options, (2) so healthy you can lose weight eating them, (3) a LOT of weight, (4) food is tasty enough to eat for months.
- **The Trojan Horse itself:** The STORY has survived 3,000+ years. The embedded lesson: "Never trust your enemies, even when they seem friendly."
- **"The Three Little Pigs":** Story of three brothers and a wolf. Embedded lesson: effort pays off, do things right.
- **Online reviews become stories:** Even when people just need to rate a product, they embed it in a personal narrative ("My son just turned eight so we were planning our first trip to Disney World...").

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify the information you want to transmit
2. Build a narrative around it that's independently shareable (funny, surprising, emotional, useful)
3. Ensure your brand/product is INTEGRAL to the story (see `[CTG:S.2]`)
4. Test: Would someone tell this story at dinner? If yes, would your brand come up naturally?

---

### [CTG:S.2] Valuable Virality

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** The MOST IMPORTANT sub-principle of Stories. There's a big difference between people talking about CONTENT and people talking about the COMPANY that created the content. Virality is only valuable when the brand is so integral to the story that people can't tell it without mentioning the brand. Critical details persist through retellings; irrelevant details drop out.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Designing any viral content or campaign; evaluating a creative idea; deciding between campaign concepts
- AVOID WHEN: N/A — this check should be applied to EVERY viral effort
- COMBINES WITH: ALL STEPPS principles (valuable virality is the meta-check for all content)

**Key Examples:**
- **FAILURE — GoldenPalace.com (Ron Bensimhon):** Streaker crashed the Athens Olympics wearing a tutu with GoldenPalace.com on his chest. Millions saw it. Everyone talked about the stunt. Nobody talked about the casino. "Like building a magnificent Trojan Horse but forgetting to put anything inside."
- **FAILURE — Evian "Roller Babies":** 50M+ views. Guinness record for most-viewed online ad. But same year: Evian lost market share and sales dropped ~25%. Roller-skating babies are cute but have nothing to do with water.
- **SUCCESS — Panda Cheese:** "Never say no to Panda." A man in a panda suit appears whenever someone says no to Panda cheese and destroys things. The brand IS the punchline — you CANNOT tell the story without mentioning Panda.
- **SUCCESS — Will It Blend? (Blendtec):** Impossible to describe the videos without talking about the blender. The product's benefit (incredible power) IS the content.
- **SUCCESS — Dove "Evolution":** Showed how much Photoshopping goes into beauty ads. Drove conversation about unrealistic beauty standards — and Dove was integral as the sponsor challenging those standards. 16M views. Double-digit sales growth.
- **SUCCESS — $100 Cheesesteak (Barclay Prime):** The story IS about the restaurant. Can't tell it without mentioning where it's from.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. **The Detachability Test:** Can people retell the story and leave out your brand? If yes → NOT valuable virality
2. **The Telephone Test:** After 5 retellings, will your brand still be mentioned? Critical details persist; irrelevant ones drop out. Allport & Postman found 70% of story details are lost in 5-6 transmissions — only core plot survives.
3. **Make brand = punchline:** Panda cheese works because the panda IS the joke. Blendtec works because the blender IS the amazement.
4. **Avoid decorative branding:** GoldenPalace.com was painted on a chest. Evian produced the video. Neither is integral to what makes the story worth telling.
5. **Build a Social Currency-laden, Triggered, Emotional, Public, Practically Valuable Trojan Horse — but don't forget to hide your message inside.**

**Reversal:** Making the brand too prominent can kill the story's organic feel. The art is making the brand ESSENTIAL but not OBNOXIOUS. People shouldn't feel like they're watching an ad — they should feel like they're hearing a great story that happens to involve your brand.

### [CTG:S.3] Stories as Cultural Learning

**Source:** Contagious (Berger), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Stories are a more efficient way to learn than trial and error, direct observation, or advertising (which isn't trusted). They provide proof by analogy — "if it happened to someone like me, it could happen to me." And people are less likely to argue against stories than advertising claims, because (1) it's hard to dispute what happened to a specific person, and (2) narrative engagement reduces cognitive resources available for counterargument.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your product benefits from third-party validation; you need to overcome skepticism; your best evidence is customer experiences
- AVOID WHEN: You need precise technical specifications (stories are vague by nature)
- COMBINES WITH: `[CTG:PV]` (stories carry practical wisdom), `[CTG:E]` (emotionally engaging stories reduce counterarguing)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Collect customer stories that naturally embed your value proposition
2. Let customers tell their OWN stories (more credible than brand-created narratives)
3. Ensure stories contain specific, vivid details (these persist through retelling)
4. Remember: one good story is worth dozens of observations and years of trial and error
