# Influence: Core Principles Reference

Source: Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion, Robert Cialdini (2021 expanded edition)

---

## [INF:RC] Reciprocation — "The Old Give and Take"
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** The rule of reciprocation states that we should try to repay what another person has provided us. This is one of the most potent levers of influence — a deeply embedded social norm across all human cultures. The obligation to reciprocate is so strong it can overpower the influence of liking: people who disliked their benefactor still bought his raffle tickets at the same rate as those who liked him (Regan experiment).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You can provide genuine value first; the target is in a position to reciprocate; you need to initiate a relationship or break through resistance; you want to establish ongoing exchange
- AVOID WHEN: The gift would be perceived as transparent bribery; the target is already hostile and will reject any gift (like Diane Louie in Jonestown); in established "communal" relationships (families, close friends) where tit-for-tat feels transactional
- COMBINES WITH: `[INF:AU]` (authority + reciprocity = 17% compliance vs 5% baseline in charity study); `[INF:LK]` (gifts from liked people are more powerful); `[INF:SC]` (scarce gift + reciprocity, as in Starbucks anniversary vouchers)
**Key Examples:**
- Regan experiment: Joe gave a 10¢ Coke, then asked subjects to buy 25¢ raffle tickets. Those who received the Coke bought twice as many tickets — a 500% ROI. Liking for Joe was irrelevant when reciprocity was activated.
- Ethiopia sent $5,000 relief aid to Mexico in 1985 because Mexico had sent aid to Ethiopia when it was invaded by Italy in 1935 — 50 years of obligation.
- McDonald's Brazil: children given a balloon on ENTRY (not exit) increased family check by 25%, including 20% increase in coffee purchases.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify what you can give FIRST that has genuine value to the recipient
2. Personalize the gift to the recipient's current needs or preferences (customized gifts are far more powerful — yogurt in a restaurant > key ring because visitors entered hungry)
3. Give before asking — the obligation must be established first
4. When thanked, don't say "No big deal." Say: "I know you'd do the same for me" (preserves the reciprocity obligation)
5. The gift doesn't have to be large — small favors produce disproportionately large return favors because people want to rid themselves of the uncomfortable feeling of indebtedness
**Defense:**
- Don't reject all gifts reflexively — that makes you a "freeloader" and isolates you socially
- Accept offers for what they FUNDAMENTALLY ARE, not what they are represented to be
- The moment you realize a "gift" was actually a compliance device (not a genuine favor), mentally redefine it: "This is a sales tactic, not a gift." The rule says favors deserve favors — tricks do not deserve favors
- Exploit the exploiter: keep the free sample, information, or inspection, then leave. Reciprocity applies to favors, not to sales schemes
**Reversal:**
- When the gift is too lavish or obviously calculated, it triggers suspicion instead of obligation ("What does he want?")
- In cultures where gift-giving is heavily regulated (government, medicine), reciprocity attempts can be illegal or unethical
- Diane Louie survived Jonestown specifically because she refused Jim Jones's special favors: "I didn't want to owe him nothin'"

### [INF:RC.1] Uninvited Debts
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** The reciprocity rule applies even to unrequested gifts. A person can trigger indebtedness by doing an uninvited favor. The obligation to receive reduces our ability to choose whom we wish to be indebted to — putting power in the giver's hands.
**Key Examples:**
- Disabled American Veterans: simple mail appeal = 18% response. Adding unsolicited personalized address labels = 35% response (nearly doubled).
- Amway's BUG: leave products at customer's home for 24-72 hours free. Customers use them and feel obligated. Average purchase: half the BUG contents.
- Hare Krishna: give a flower in the airport before asking for donation. People who accepted felt obligated even though they never wanted the flower.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Provide something of value before the person has a chance to decline
2. Frame it as a gift, not merchandise — "There is a strong cultural pressure to reciprocate a gift, but no such pressure to purchase an unwanted product"
3. The gift works even when the recipient doesn't particularly like what was given — the obligation is to return the favor, not to value the gift itself

### [INF:RC.2] Personalization via Customization
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Gifts personalized to the recipient's current needs or preferences supercharge reciprocity. Problem-freed feels better than problem-free — a service stumble that is immediately corrected produces higher satisfaction than a flawless experience.
**Key Examples:**
- Fast-food restaurant: warm greeting + key ring gift = 12% more food purchased. Warm greeting + yogurt cup (same retail value, but matched to food need) = 24% more food purchased.
- Hotel GM purchased tennis racquets for a guest's children within 20 minutes. Mother booked her entire extended family for July 4th weekend.
- Consultant sent personalized modern-art postcards (matching client's collecting interest) with invoices — payment time went from 6 months to near-immediate.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Learn the recipient's current needs, preferences, or pain points BEFORE giving
2. Match the gift to what they need RIGHT NOW (food for hungry people, solution for frustrated people)
3. Service recovery is a form of personalized reciprocity — when you fix a problem, the fix is perceived as a personalized gift
4. "Problem-free may not feel as good to people as problem-freed"

### [INF:RC.3] Rejection-Then-Retreat (Door-in-the-Face)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Make a large request that will be refused, then retreat to your actual (smaller) request. The retreat is perceived as a concession, which triggers reciprocal concession from the target. This combines the reciprocity rule WITH the perceptual contrast principle — the smaller request looks even smaller by comparison.
**Key Examples:**
- Boy Scout: asked Cialdini for $5 circus tickets (refused), then retreated to $1 chocolate bars. Cialdini bought two despite not liking chocolate and not wanting to spend money.
- Zoo trip experiment: asking students to be unpaid chaperons for juvenile delinquents at the zoo = 17% compliance. Asking first for 2 years of counseling (refused), then retreating to zoo trip = 50% compliance (3x increase).
- Watergate: Liddy's first proposal was $1 million (kidnapping, call girls, chase plane). After two rejections, his "bare-bones" $250,000 break-in plan was approved. "A quarter of a million dollars had come to be a little something."
**Sweet Side Effects:**
- Greater responsibility: targets feel they "dictated" the final agreement and are more likely to follow through (85% show-up vs 50%)
- Greater satisfaction: targets feel more satisfied with the outcome, making them willing to agree to future requests (84% vs 43% in blood donation study)
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Start with a request larger than what you actually want — but NOT so extreme it seems unreasonable (the Bar-Ilan study showed that absurd initial requests backfire)
2. After the refusal, present your actual request as a "concession"
3. The target should view the retreat as genuine bargaining in good faith
4. Use for negotiations: open high, concede systematically — the opponent feels they "won" concessions
5. Structure: heads I win (they accept the big request), tails you lose (they feel obligated to accept the small one)
**Defense:**
- Recognize the setup: if a large request is followed immediately by a smaller one from the same person, you may be in a rejection-then-retreat sequence
- Redefine the concession: it's a tactic, not a genuine compromise. You owe no reciprocal concession to a contrived retreat

### [INF:RC.4] Unequal Exchanges
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** The rule demands only that one sort of action be reciprocated with a SIMILAR sort of action — but allows considerable flexibility. A small favor can create obligation for a substantially larger return favor. People agree to unequal exchanges to rid themselves of the uncomfortable feeling of indebtedness and to avoid being labeled "moocher" or "taker."
**Key Examples:**
- Regan experiment: a 10¢ Coke produced purchase of 50¢+ in raffle tickets — 500% return.
- Student lent her new car to a virtual stranger who once jump-started her car. He totaled it.
- CIA officer gave Viagra to Afghan tribal patriarch with four younger wives; in return got "a bonanza of information about Taliban movements and supply routes."

## [INF:LK] Liking — "The Friendly Thief"
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** People prefer to say yes to individuals they like. Compliance professionals systematically exploit multiple factors that produce liking: physical attractiveness, similarity, compliments, familiarity/contact, cooperation, and positive association. The power of liking was demonstrated by Tupperware parties where the social bond between hostess and guests was TWICE as likely to determine purchases as preference for the product itself.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You need to build rapport before making a request; you're in a sales or negotiation context; you need to overcome initial resistance; you want to build a referral network
- AVOID WHEN: The relationship is already established and trust is the issue (shift to Authority); when liking without substance will be seen as manipulative; in urgent situations where relationship-building time is unavailable
- COMBINES WITH: `[INF:RC]` (gifts from liked people are even more compelling); `[INF:UN]` (liking is "like us," unity is "one of us" — unity is stronger); `[INF:SP]` (a recommendation from a liked friend is the most trusted form of social proof — 92% trust per Nielsen)
**Key Examples:**
- Joe Girard, "Greatest Car Salesman" (Guinness): sold 5+ cars/trucks per day for 12 years. Formula: fair price + someone they liked. Sent 13,000+ "I like you" cards per month.
- George Clooney endorsing evolution: people who learned Clooney supported evolutionary theory became more accepting — regardless of age, sex, or religiosity. Emma Watson had the same effect.
- Malpractice attorney Alice Burkin: "People just don't sue doctors they like."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify which liking sub-factors are available in your situation (see below)
2. Build genuine rapport BEFORE making any request
3. In Tupperware-party style: leverage existing friendships (friend makes the request, not the salesperson)
4. Use the "endless chain" referral method: ask satisfied customers for names of friends
**Defense:**
- Don't try to prevent liking from developing — it happens unconsciously through too many channels
- Instead, use TIMING: notice when you like a compliance practitioner MORE than you should under the circumstances
- When you detect undue liking, mentally separate the PERSON from the OFFER: "I'm buying the car, not the salesman"
- Ask yourself: "In the 45 minutes I've known this person, have I come to like them more than expected?" If yes, focus exclusively on the deal's merits
**Reversal:**
- Manufactured liking (fake similarities, hollow compliments) can backfire if detected — especially with sophisticated audiences
- Liking without competence or substance produces "nice but unhelpful" perception

### [INF:LK.1] Physical Attractiveness
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Physical attractiveness creates a halo effect — we automatically assign good-looking people favorable traits (talent, kindness, honesty, intelligence) and do so without realizing it. Attractive people get more votes (2.5x), higher pay ($230K more over career), more help, lighter sentences, and better hiring outcomes.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Grooming and appearance matter disproportionately — job interview study showed grooming mattered MORE than qualifications
2. Sales training should include grooming/appearance guidance
3. This is a "click, run" response — it operates automatically, which means people underestimate its effect on them

### [INF:LK.2] Similarity
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** We like people who are like us — in opinions, personality traits, background, lifestyle, clothing, name, even fingerprint patterns. This works even when the similarity is trivial. Mimicry of body language and speech patterns increases liking and compliance.
**Key Examples:**
- Survey completion nearly doubled when the survey-taker's name was similar to the recipient's (Robert Greer → Bob Gregar; Cynthia Johnston → Cindy Johanson).
- 421 million dating matches: similarity was the #1 predictor of favorability.
- Food servers who mimicked customers' words received higher tips. Salespeople who mirrored verbal/nonverbal behavior sold more electronics. Negotiators who imitated opponents got better results across cultures.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Find genuine commonalities — don't fake them
2. Mirror language, style, and energy of your audience
3. Use the same words and phrases your audience uses (per Psychology for Marketers advice)
4. In negotiations: find and reference shared interests BEFORE bargaining ("Oh, you're a runner; I'm a runner!")
5. Caution: people naturally focus on differences rather than similarities — deliberately look for connections

### [INF:LK.3] Compliments
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Flattery works even when the target knows it's flattery and even when it's inaccurate. "I like you" sent 150,000+ times per year on printed cards still sold cars. Pure praise produced just as much liking for the flatterer when untrue as when true.
**Key Examples:**
- Joe Girard's 13,000+ "I like you" monthly cards — impersonal, obviously designed to sell, yet effective.
- North Carolina experiment: people liked the flatterer best even when they knew he had ulterior motives and even when praise was inaccurate.
- Restaurant servers who said "You made a good choice" got larger tips. Hair stylists who said "Any hairstyle would look good on you" did too.
**Advanced Tactics:**
- Give compliments BEHIND someone's back — they reach the person without the appearance of self-serving motives
- Praise the TRAIT, not just the behavior: "You're a conscientious person" instead of "Good job on that report" — this creates a reputation to live up to (altercasting)
- Newspaper carrier Carl: praised for "conscientiousness" in paper placement → went from 75% accuracy to 100%

### [INF:LK.4] Contact and Cooperation
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Familiarity through repeated contact increases liking — but ONLY under positive conditions. Contact under negative conditions (competition, conflict) decreases liking. This is why school desegregation alone didn't reduce prejudice — the classroom's competitive structure made things worse. Cooperation toward shared goals is the key.
**Key Examples:**
- Sherif's Robbers Cave: boys separated into rival groups → hostility. Joint tasks requiring cooperation (stuck truck, water pipe, pooling money for movie) → friendships across groups.
- Aronson's Jigsaw Classroom: students who must teach each other to pass tests → less prejudice, higher minority self-esteem, better test scores for everyone.
- Good Cop/Bad Cop: works because Good Cop creates an illusion of cooperation — "someone on his side, working together with him."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Create situations where you and the target must cooperate toward a shared goal
2. Don't just spend time together — work together on something meaningful
3. Position yourself as a teammate, not an adversary
4. In sales: "do battle" with the manager on behalf of the customer (Gary the car salesman)

### [INF:LK.5] Association
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** We are affected by things merely CONNECTED to a person or product. Good associations transfer positive feelings; bad associations transfer negative ones. People "bask in reflected glory" (BIRG) by connecting to winners and distance from losers ("We won" vs "They lost").
**Key Examples:**
- Credit card logos in a room: people spent 29% more on catalog items; donated 87% vs 33% to charity — even when paying cash.
- Cars in ads with attractive models: rated faster, more appealing, more expensive — men denied the model influenced them.
- Gregory Razran's luncheon technique: political statements shown during eating gained approval. Food = positive feelings = positive association.
- Sports fans: "We're number one!" after wins. "They lost" after defeats. Arizona State students used "we" for wins, "they" for losses.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Associate your product/request with things people already feel positive about
2. Deliver bad news through others; deliver good news yourself
3. Create positive experiences during your interaction (food, pleasant environment)
4. Don't link yourself to negative events — the "horns effect" (bearer of bad news) is real

## [INF:SP] Social Proof — "Truths Are Us"
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** We determine what is correct by finding out what other people think is correct. We view an action as more appropriate to the degree that we see others performing it. This works because, most of the time, following the crowd IS the right thing to do. Three optimizing conditions: uncertainty, the many, and similarity.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your audience is uncertain about what to do; you have evidence of popularity, high adoption, or peer behavior; you need to overcome "first mover" hesitation; you want to normalize a desired behavior
- AVOID WHEN: The social proof points in the WRONG direction (don't publicize how many people are doing the undesirable thing — the "Big Mistake"); you lack genuine evidence and would need to fabricate it; in highly individualistic decisions where peer behavior is irrelevant
- COMBINES WITH: `[INF:AU]` (expert endorsement + popularity = reduced uncertainty); `[INF:LK]` (recommendation from a liked person who is also "one of many" is most trusted); `[INF:UN]` (social proof from in-group members is most powerful)
**Key Examples:**
- Beijing restaurants: labeling dishes "most popular" increased sales 13-20%. Best effect on infrequent (unfamiliar) visitors.
- McDonald's McFlurry: "The McFlurry is our visitors' favorite" = 55% sales jump. Adding "The [x] flavor is our visitors' favorite topping" = additional 48% increase.
- Netflix: internal tests showed that telling members which shows were popular made those shows MORE popular. They reversed their long-standing secrecy policy.
- Sylvan Goldman and the shopping cart: customers wouldn't use his invention until he hired shoppers to wheel carts through the store. Then everyone followed.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Make popularity VISIBLE — label items "most popular," show adoption numbers, display testimonials
2. Use the three optimizers: uncertainty (works best on unfamiliar audiences), the many (more people = more proof), and similarity (peer behavior > authority behavior)
3. NEVER publicize undesirable behavior's frequency — it normalizes it (the "Big Mistake")
4. If you don't have existing social proof, use FUTURE social proof: "trending," "growing fastest"
5. Social proof information is MORE persuasive when it seems unintentional — Toyota recruitment ads that mentioned high demand increased car sales by 41.7%
**Defense:**
- Watch for counterfeit social proof: canned laughter, seeded tip jars, fake reviews, fabricated "unrehearsed interviews"
- Spot fake online reviews: lack of detail, excess first-person pronouns, more verbs than nouns
- Recognize pluralistic ignorance: in emergencies, everyone looking at everyone else and seeing no one react → everyone concludes nothing is wrong
- In emergencies: point to ONE specific person and give clear instructions ("You in the blue jacket, call 911")
**Reversal:**
- Social proof backfires when used to highlight the PROBLEM: "Your heritage is being vandalized every day by theft losses of 14 tons a year" → theft nearly tripled. Instead, marginalize the bad behavior and spotlight the good majority.
- After a publicized suicide, copycat suicides increase — especially among similar people (same age). Media coverage of mass killings spawns imitative violence.

### [INF:SP.1] The Many — Validity, Feasibility, Social Acceptance
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** The more people doing something, the more powerful the social proof. Three reasons: (1) Validity — if many do it, it's probably correct. (2) Feasibility — if many can do it, I probably can too. (3) Social Acceptance — being one of many feels safe; dissenting feels painful (amygdala activation).
**Key Examples:**
- Street-gazing experiment: one person looking up = few join. Bring 20 friends = 80% of passersby look up.
- Energy conservation: "Most of your neighbors conserve energy" produced 3.5x more savings than environmental, social responsibility, or cost-saving messages — and homeowners predicted it would be LEAST effective.
- British shopping mall: poster with single early-luncher = 25% increase. Poster with multiple = 75% increase.

### [INF:SP.2] Similarity — Peer-suasion
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** We follow the lead of SIMILAR others most powerfully. The behavior of peers gives us the greatest insight into correct behavior for ourselves. Peer-suasion is so powerful it extends to life-and-death decisions.
**Key Examples:**
- Chris learning to swim: his father and a swimming instructor couldn't get him to swim without his ring. Three-year-old Tommy who could swim without a ring = Chris abandoned his ring instantly. "Tommy is three, I'm three, so I can too."
- David Phillips's Werther effect: publicized suicides increase suicide rate. Young person's suicide → young drivers die in crashes. Older person's suicide → older drivers die.
- Bible sales: using female customer names with female prospects, male with male, couples with couples = 119.67% sales improvement.

### [INF:SP.3] Uncertainty
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** When people are unsure what to do — when the situation is ambiguous — they are MOST susceptible to social proof. This is when they look to others for guidance. Pluralistic ignorance occurs when everyone is looking at everyone else for cues, sees no one reacting, and concludes nothing is wrong.
**Key Examples:**
- Bystander effect: Lee Alexis Wilson murdered near the Art Institute in Chicago. "One man heard a scream but did not investigate because no one else seemed to be paying attention."
- Jonestown mass suicide: in the alien Guyanese environment, members had no one to look to except each other. Jones's relocation strategy was his "real genius" — in Guyana, social proof pointed exclusively toward compliance.

## [INF:AU] Authority — "Directed Deference"
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** People defer to authority figures to a remarkable degree — often automatically and unconsciously. Authority comes in two forms: being IN authority (positional power, boss) and being AN authority (expertise, knowledge). The second type is far more effective because people are usually willing, even eager, to follow recommendations from someone who knows more. The most influential authority is a CREDIBLE one: expert + trustworthy.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You need to overcome skepticism with expertise; you want to establish trust quickly; you're making recommendations that require the audience to change established behaviors; you need to reduce uncertainty
- AVOID WHEN: You lack genuine credentials relevant to the topic; using authority symbols (titles, clothes) without substance; the audience is highly expert themselves and will see through shallow authority claims
- COMBINES WITH: `[INF:RC]` (authority + gift = 17% compliance vs 12% for authority alone and 11% for gift alone in the UK banker study); `[INF:CC]` (authority-endorsed commitments are more sticky); `[INF:SP]` (multiple experts > single expert)
**Key Examples:**
- Milgram experiments: 65% of ordinary people delivered maximum (450V) shocks when ordered by a lab-coated authority. Sex was irrelevant. Even when victim screamed about heart trouble.
- Hospital nurses: 95% complied with unauthorized phone orders from an unknown "doctor" to administer double the maximum dose of an unapproved drug — violating 4 separate protocols.
- "Rectal earache": doctor wrote "R ear" → nurse put ear drops in patient's rectum. Neither nurse nor patient questioned it.
- Bank security hack: 96% success rate. Hackers used fire-inspector and government-monitor uniforms to walk into restricted areas.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Establish expertise FIRST — diplomas, certifications, credentials, experience
2. Then establish trustworthiness — mention a weakness or mistake BEFORE presenting strengths
3. Use multiple experts: "Audiences trust a set of experts more than any one of them"
4. Just ONE expert Op-Ed piece lifts agreement by 20 percentage points
5. Don't confuse being IN authority (which generates resentment) with being AN authority (which generates willing compliance)
**Defense:**
- Ask TWO questions: "Is this authority truly an expert?" (focuses on real credentials, not symbols) and "How truthful can I expect this expert to be?" (looks for self-interest)
- Watch for fake authority: actors playing doctors ("I'm not a doctor, but I play one on TV" — Vicks Formula 44 ad was hugely successful)
- People UNDERESTIMATE authority's effect: students in Bickman's uniform study guessed 63% compliance; actual was 92%
- Be especially wary of the "argue against own interest" trustworthiness tactic — it's often used deceptively (Vincent the waiter)
**Reversal:**
- "Captainitis" in aviation: copilots defer to captains even when the captain makes an obvious error → crashes
- Command-and-control authority generates resistance and resentment — use informational authority instead

### [INF:AU.1] Symbols of Authority — Titles, Clothes, Trappings
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** We respond to the SYMBOLS of authority as readily as to its substance. Three symbols: titles (easiest to fake, hardest to earn), clothes (uniforms, business suits), and trappings (expensive cars, jewelry). People consistently underestimate the influence of these symbols.
**Key Examples:**
- Title experiment: same man introduced as "student" to "professor" — grew 2.5 inches in perceived height with each status increase.
- Bickman's security guard: in uniform, nearly all obeyed odd requests. In street clothes, less than half. The uniform's power lasted even after the requester left.
- Texas jaywalking: 3.5x more pedestrians followed a suited jaywalker into traffic than a casually dressed one.
- Prestige car at stoplight: 50% waited behind luxury car without honking. Nearly all honked at economy car; two rammed its bumper.

### [INF:AU.2] Credible Authority — Expertise + Trustworthiness
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** A CREDIBLE authority possesses two features: expertise (relevant knowledge) AND trustworthiness (honest presentation). To establish trustworthiness rapidly, mention a weakness early — then the strengths that follow are believed more deeply.
**Key Examples:**
- Warren Buffett: near the start of every annual report, describes a mistake or problem FIRST — even in a banner year (2016), he detailed the 1993 Dexter Shoe fiasco. This makes all subsequent positive claims more credible.
- Domino's "NEW DOMINO'S" campaign: admitted past pizza quality was poor → sales sky high, stock price soared.
- Vincent the waiter: recommended LESS expensive dishes to the first orderer, establishing himself as honest → then upsold wine and desserts to the entire table with enormous credibility.
- Trial attorneys who admit a weakness BEFORE the rival points it out are viewed as more credible and win more often.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Mention a weakness or prior mistake EARLY in your message (first page of report, first minute of pitch)
2. Immediately follow with your strongest point — it will be processed most deeply because it comes from a now-trustworthy source
3. The weakness should be real but secondary — easily overcome by more significant advantages
4. Examples: "We're #2. We try harder" (Avis). "We're more expensive, and you're worth it" (L'Oréal).
5. Five-star reviews are LESS trusted than 4.2-4.7 range. Presence of negative reviews increases conversion by 67%.
