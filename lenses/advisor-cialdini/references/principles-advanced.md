Source: Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion, Robert Cialdini (2021 expanded edition)

## [INF:SC] Scarcity — "The Rule of the Few"
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** People assign more value to opportunities that are less available. This operates through two mechanisms: (1) scarcity as a shortcut to quality — difficult to get = typically better; (2) loss aversion — people are more motivated by the thought of LOSING something than gaining something of equal value (Kahneman's prospect theory). The principle applies to commodities, information, and freedoms.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have genuine limited supply or limited time; the audience is already interested but hesitant; you need to motivate immediate action; you're competing for attention in a crowded market; you have exclusive information
- AVOID WHEN: You have no genuine scarcity (fabricating it is exploitation); the audience hasn't yet decided they WANT the item (deploy relationship-building and uncertainty-reduction principles first); the scarcity claim is absurd enough to destroy credibility
- COMBINES WITH: `[INF:CC]` (get commitment FIRST, then add scarcity pressure to motivate action); `[INF:SP]` (scarcity caused by social demand is the most powerful kind); `[INF:AU]` (exclusive information from an authority source = "scarcity double whammy")
**Key Examples:**
- Sandy the divorce mediator: changing "All you have to do is agree to this proposal, and we will have a deal" to "We have a deal. All you have to do is agree to this proposal" — worked "every time" because it framed as losing an already-possessed deal.
- iPhone 5 line: woman traded $2,800 Louis Vuitton bag for a spot closer to the front — "I didn't want to risk LOSING the chance to get one."
- Beef import study: standard pitch = baseline. Scarce-supply info = 2x sales. Scarce-supply as EXCLUSIVE information = 6x sales.
- Coca-Cola's New Coke fiasco: during taste tests, the unavailable New Coke was preferred by extra 6%. After launch, the now-unavailable OLD Coke became the object of passionate demand. Company misread their own data.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Frame offers in terms of what will be LOST, not what will be gained: "We have a deal" before mentioning the condition, not after
2. Use limited-number tactics honestly: "Only 3 left at this price"
3. Use limited-time tactics: deadlines, countdown timers, "exclusive limited engagement ends soon"
4. Highlight what's UNIQUE about your offering that can't be obtained elsewhere — if you don't have limited supply, you may have limited features
5. Make information scarce: exclusive news, insider access, "not generally available" data
6. NEW scarcity is more powerful than constant scarcity — something going from available to unavailable is more desirable than something that was always scarce
7. Competition for scarce resources amplifies the effect enormously — schedule prospects simultaneously (Richard's used-car technique)
**Defense:**
- Use the arousal itself as your warning signal: whenever you feel the rush of excitement about a scarce item, STOP and ask: "Why do I want this?"
- Two-stage defense: (1) calm the arousal, (2) ask: "Do I want this for OWNING it or for USING it?"
- If for using: remember "the scarce cookies didn't taste any better." Scarcity changes desire, not quality.
- If for owning: then scarcity IS a legitimate factor in pricing — the less available, the more valuable as a possession.
**Reversal:**
- Scarcity pressure without prior relationship or trust = pushy, desperate, repellent
- False scarcity that's discovered destroys all credibility (Best Buy employees caught faking "last one" claims)
- Over-scarcity on low-value items seems absurd ("Only 2 left!" on a commodity product)

### [INF:SC.1] Limited Numbers
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Informing a customer that supply is limited creates urgency. Research shows limited-supply appeals are generally more effective than limited-time appeals. Booking.com's first inclusion of "rooms remaining at this price" produced purchases so high the customer-service team reported "what must be a systems error."
**Key Examples:**
- Appliance store: salesman tells couple the item was sold to another couple 20 minutes ago. Customer commits to buy at full price if one can be found in the back. Salesman "finds" one. Purchase decision was made at the moment of maximum scarcity.
- Resale shop: woman couldn't sell a set of collectibles together. Brought each one separately — store took every one. Scarcity of individual items vs abundance of the set.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Show real inventory counts: "Only X left in stock"
2. Don't present abundant items in abundance — it reduces perceived value
3. If you can't limit supply, limit the unique FEATURES or COMBINATIONS

### [INF:SC.2] Limited Time (Deadlines)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Time limits generate urgency. The "deadline" tactic places an official time limit on the customer's opportunity. The most aggressive version: "This offer is only good right now" (the "can't come back" claim). The real purpose: "keep the prospects from taking the time to think the deal over by scaring them into believing they can't have it later, which makes them want it now."
**Key Examples:**
- Vacuum cleaner sales: "I have so many other people to see that I have the time to visit a family only once. It's company policy that, even if you decide later, I can't come back."
- Movie ads compressing three scarcity signals into five words: "Exclusive, limited engagement ends soon!"
- Child-portrait photography: "We must burn the unsold pictures of your children within 24 hours."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Set real deadlines for offers — countdown timers, expiration dates
2. The deadline must feel genuine — obviously fake urgency repels
3. Limited-time is less effective than limited-supply, so combine when possible

### [INF:SC.3] Competition for Scarce Resources
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** When we are in COMPETITION with others for a scarce resource, desire intensifies dramatically. The combination of scarcity + rivalry is the "devilish construction" that produces the most irrational behavior. This is why auctions, bidding wars, and "another buyer is interested" tactics work.
**Key Examples:**
- Richard's used-car technique: scheduled all buyers for the same time. First buyer sees second arrive → "now-or-never" urgency. Third arrives → trap snaps shut. Never failed to sell at asking price.
- ABC's Poseidon Adventure: first-ever open-bid auction for a movie produced $3.3 million (expected $1M loss). CBS president Robert Wood: "The fever of the thing caught us. Like a guy who had lost his mind, I kept bidding."
- Romantic rivals: "The ardor of an indifferent lover surges with the appearance of a rival."
- Real estate: "goosing 'em off the fence" — tell hesitant buyer another prospect is coming back tomorrow.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Make competition visible: waiting lists, "X people are looking at this right now"
2. Use loss leaders to create a "feeding frenzy" — then offer other items to the aroused crowd
3. Schedule potential buyers simultaneously
4. The losing bidder should be the one smiling — the winner often overpaid

### [INF:SC.4] Psychological Reactance
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** When freedoms are restricted, people want them MORE — and want the goods/services connected to them more too. Most visible at ages 2 and in teenage years (both characterized by emerging sense of individuality). Banning information makes people want it more AND believe it more, even before receiving it.
**Key Examples:**
- Terrible twos experiment: boys went to obstructed toy 3x faster than unobstructed toy.
- Romeo and Juliet effect: parental interference with teenage couples increased love intensity. When interference weakened, romantic feelings cooled.
- Dade County phosphate ban: smuggling, hoarding, AND elevated ratings of banned detergents (gentler, more effective, better whiteners).
- University of North Carolina: banned speech opposing coed dorms → students became MORE opposed to coed dorms without ever hearing the speech.
- Jury instructions to disregard inadmissible evidence often INCREASE its weight in jurors' minds.
**"But You Are Free" Counter-technique:**
- Adding "But you are free to decline/refuse/say no" to any request often MORE THAN DOUBLES compliance across 42 experiments. It works because it reduces reactance by reestablishing the recipient's freedom to choose.

### [INF:SC.5] New Scarcity vs. Constant Scarcity
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Items that have RECENTLY become less available are valued more than items that have always been scarce. The drop from abundance to scarcity is the critical trigger. This explains revolutions (Davies's theory): periods of progress followed by reversal produce rebellion — not constant deprivation.
**Key Examples:**
- Cookie experiment: cookies moving from jar of 10 to jar of 2 = rated higher than cookies always in jar of 2.
- American Revolution: colonists had the highest standard of living and lowest taxes in the Western world — revolted only when British sought to REDUCE prosperity.
- 1991 Soviet coup: Gorbachev granted new freedoms, plotters tried to revoke them → massive popular resistance in 3 days.
- Parenting: inconsistent enforcement creates established freedoms that are then "taken away" → rebellion. Consistent enforcement avoids this.

## [INF:CC] Commitment & Consistency — "Hobgoblins of the Mind"
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** Once we make a choice or take a stand, we encounter personal and interpersonal pressures to think and behave consistently with that commitment. We have a deep desire to be (and appear) consistent. This tendency serves us well most of the time (inconsistency = confused, two-faced; consistency = rational, stable, trustworthy) but can be exploited. The most effective commitments are: active, public, effortful, and freely chosen (internally motivated).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You need lasting behavior change, not just one-time compliance; you want to build loyalty (Amazon's "Pay to Quit"); you're designing onboarding flows, pledges, or registration processes; you want someone to follow through on a verbal agreement
- AVOID WHEN: The commitment feels forced or coerced (reduces inner responsibility and backfires); the initial commitment is clearly irrelevant to the later request (transparent manipulation); in collectivist cultures where personal consistency matters less than group harmony
- COMBINES WITH: `[INF:SC]` (commit first, then add urgency to act); `[INF:SP]` (public commitment + social proof of others doing same = reinforcing); `[INF:AU]` (expert-endorsed commitment is stickier)
**Key Examples:**
- Amazon's "Pay to Quit": offers $5,000 to leave. Very few take it. Bezos wants employees "to take a moment and think about what they really want" — the decision to stay deepens commitment and productivity.
- Horse-track bettors: confidence in their choice increases SIGNIFICANTLY just after placing the bet. Nothing about the horse changes.
- Toy manufacturers' undersupply strategy: advertise special toy → parents promise → stores sold out → buy substitutes for Christmas → toy reappears in January → "You promised, you promised" → parents buy AGAIN.
- Steven Sherman's prediction technique: calling residents and asking "Would you volunteer for the American Cancer Society?" → 700% increase in actual volunteers when called later.
- "How are you feeling tonight?" technique: 89% of those who said "Fine" then agreed to buy cookies for Hunger Relief (vs 18% standard).
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Get a small initial commitment (foot-in-the-door) — even a trivial one changes self-image
2. Make commitments active (written > verbal > mental), public (visible to others), effortful (harder = more binding), and freely chosen (no strong external pressure)
3. Reduce first-page form fields to create "started" feeling — dropping from 4 to 3 fields increases registration completions by 50%
4. Ask people to predict their behavior — the prediction itself becomes a commitment
5. Use reminders of past commitments — they regenerate AND intensify the commitment's power
**Defense:**
- Listen to your STOMACH: when you feel a knot of "I'm being trapped," that's the signal
- Listen to your HEART OF HEARTS: "Knowing what I now know, if I could go back in time, would I make the same choice?" Trust the FIRST flash of feeling before rationalizations kick in
- Call it out: "I recognize this as a consistency trap. I refuse to be locked into a mechanical sequence."
- Recognize that commitments "grow their own legs" — new justifications sprout to support a choice, even after the original reason is gone (Sara and Tim)
**Reversal:**
- Commitments made under strong external pressure (large rewards, threats) DON'T change self-image — they produce only temporary compliance
- "Foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds" (Emerson) — consistency for its own sake is not wisdom
- People with strong preference for consistency are especially vulnerable — particularly older adults (>50) and members of individualistic societies

### [INF:CC.1] Foot-in-the-Door
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** Start with a small request to gain eventual compliance with larger ones. The small action changes self-image: "I'm the kind of person who does this sort of thing." Then consistency pressures drive compliance with related larger requests — even ones on different topics.
**Key Examples:**
- Freedman & Fraser: signing a "Keep California Beautiful" petition → 50% agreed to huge "DRIVE CAREFULLY" billboard on their lawn (vs 17% baseline). Different topic, 2 weeks apart.
- Chinese POW camps: "The United States is not perfect" → list problems → sign name → read to group → radio broadcast → full "collaborator" identity.
- Charitable organizations: agree to interview → "momentum of compliance" → organ/bone-marrow donation.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Start with a request so small nearly everyone agrees
2. Ensure the initial action is ACTIVE (they do something, not just fail to object)
3. The initial commitment need not be on the exact same topic — it shifts self-image broadly
4. Be very careful about signing petitions — they change your self-concept

### [INF:CC.2] Active and Public Commitments
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** Written commitments are more powerful than verbal; public commitments more than private. Writing provides physical evidence that can't be denied. Public commitments create social pressure to maintain the stand. Deutsch & Gerard: publicly committed students were most resistant to changing their answers, even when shown they were wrong.
**Key Examples:**
- Chinese essay contests: small prizes (few cigarettes) for pro-Communist essays. Why small prizes? So prisoners would OWN the commitment — no external justification.
- Amway: "Set a goal and write it down. There is something magical about writing things down."
- Door-to-door sales: having the CUSTOMER fill out the sales agreement (not the salesperson) dramatically reduced cooling-off cancellations.
- Restaurant no-shows: changing "Please call us if you change your plans" to "Will you please call us?" + pause for answer → no-shows dropped from 30% to 10%.
- Weight loss: MyFitnessPal users who exposed calorie counts to friends lost 50% more weight.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Get it in writing — have them fill out the form, write the goal, sign the pledge
2. Make it public — social media shares, team announcements, visible pledges
3. For behavior change: combine written + public + social network visibility
4. Subway's Fred DeLuca: printed "10,000 stores by 2001" on every napkin — "If I put my goals down in writing and make them known to the world, I'm committed."

### [INF:CC.3] Effort and Initiation
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** The more effort that goes into a commitment, the greater its ability to influence attitudes and actions. This explains why harsh initiation rites (fraternity hazing, military boot camp, tribal ceremonies) persist despite attempts to ban them — they produce fierce loyalty. "Persons who go through a great deal of trouble or pain to attain something tend to value it more highly."
**Key Examples:**
- Thonga tribe initiation: beatings, cold, thirst, unsavory food, punishment, death threats — produces lifelong tribal loyalty.
- Aronson & Mills: women who endured severe embarrassment to join a discussion group rated the (deliberately boring) group as extremely valuable.
- Hangzhou waste separation: residents who had to work HARDER to separate trash then also reduced electricity consumption more — effort deepened environmental commitment broadly.
- William Styron on Marine boot camp: "There is no ex-Marine who does not view the training as a crucible out of which he emerged more resilient, braver and better for the wear."

### [INF:CC.4] Inner Choice (No Strong External Pressure)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** We accept inner responsibility for behavior when we think we chose it WITHOUT strong outside pressure. Large rewards or strong threats produce compliance but NOT inner commitment — and when removed, the behavior stops. This is why Chinese POWs wrote essays for tiny prizes and why fraternities refuse to substitute community service for hazing — they need members to OWN the commitment.
**Key Examples:**
- Freedman's forbidden-toy experiment: boys threatened with severe punishment → 77% played with robot 6 weeks later. Boys given only "It's wrong to play with the robot" (no threat) → only 33% played with it later. The mild instruction created internalized belief; the threat created only temporary compliance.
- Child-rearing implication: "never heavily bribe or threaten children to do things we want them truly to believe in" — use the minimum pressure needed to produce the behavior
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Provide just enough reason to produce the initial behavior — but NOT so much that it explains away the choice
2. Avoid large rewards or threats for the desired commitment — they undermine self-attribution
3. After someone commits, don't remind them of the external incentive — let them generate their own reasons

### [INF:CC.5] Low-Ball Technique
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** Offer an advantage to get a decision, then remove the advantage. The decision persists because it has "grown its own legs" — new justifications emerge to support the choice that wouldn't have existed without the initial favorable offer. Works because commitment triggers self-reinforcing rationalization.
**Key Examples:**
- Car dealerships: offer a price $700 below competitors to get the commitment. Then discover an "error" or the boss "cancels the deal." Customer pays $700 more because they've already committed.
- Sara and Tim: Tim promised to stop drinking and marry her. She chose him. He reneged on both promises. She became MORE devoted — the commitment had grown its own legs.
- Iowa energy conservation: homeowners told their names would be in the paper → saved 12.2% gas. Letter said no publicity → they saved 15.5% MORE, because the publicity reason was removed and only internal motivation remained.
- Cialdini's 7 AM experiment: asked students to participate in a study (56% said yes), then mentioned 7 AM start time → none withdrew, 95% showed up.

### [INF:CC.6] Cueing Consistency — Reminders
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** Simple reminders of past commitments regenerate their power AND intensify the related self-image. Physicians reminded of patient-safety commitment washed hands 45% more. Doctors who displayed their signed anti-overscription pledge cut inappropriate prescribing by 27%. Consumers reminded of past pro-environmental actions came to see themselves as MORE environmentally minded and then purchased greener products.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Remind people of commitments they've already made
2. The reminder doesn't need to be heavy-handed — a visual cue, a reference to past actions
3. Reminders both restore AND strengthen the commitment — they're cumulative

## [INF:UN] Unity — "The 'We' Is the Shared Me"
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** People say yes to someone they consider one of them. Unity is NOT about similarity ("like us") — it's about shared identity ("one OF us"). It's about tribe-like categories: race, ethnicity, nationality, family, political and religious affiliations. Within "we"-groups, members favor fellow members' outcomes, follow their preferences, and sacrifice for the group. "Tribalism is human nature." Three constants: favoritism for in-group outcomes, tendency to follow in-group behavior, and evolutionary advantage of both.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You can establish genuine shared identity with the target; you're speaking to an audience that identifies as a group; you want deep loyalty, not just transactional compliance; you need to overcome resistance that logic and liking alone can't breach
- AVOID WHEN: The shared identity claim is fraudulent (affinity fraud like Madoff's scheme); you're trying to exploit in-group loyalty for unethical purposes; the unity claim would seem forced or pandering
- COMBINES WITH: `[INF:LK]` (liking = "like us"; unity = "one of us" — unity is stronger and operates at a deeper level); `[INF:SP]` (social proof from in-group members is the most powerful form); `[INF:CC]` (commitment to in-group identity drives consistent group-supporting behavior)
**Key Examples:**
- Ali Reda dethroning Joe Girard as world's greatest car salesman: same techniques as Girard, but Reda was Arab American selling in Dearborn (largest Arab American community in the US). Ethnic "we"-ness was the differentiator.
- Bernard Madoff's Ponzi scheme: Jewish swindler targeting Jewish investors via Jewish lieutenants — "affinity scheme" exploiting shared ethnic identity.
- Rabbi Kalisch saving Jews in Japanese territory by telling Japanese officers "We are Asian, like you" — shifted their in-group from Axis alliance to racial/regional identity.
- Warren Buffett's 2015 letter: "I will tell you what I would say to my family today if they asked about Berkshire's future." "Buffett had me at FAMILY."
- Nazi guard sparing one prisoner from execution line: recognized him as from his hometown.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify the strongest shared identity category with your audience (family, locale, ethnicity, profession, values)
2. RAISE that identity to consciousness — don't assume people are already thinking about it. "Partnership-raising" in couples: simply saying "We've been together for a while and we care for each other" was the ONLY approach that worked (coercion backfired, logic was dismissed)
3. Use familial language: "brothers," "sisterhood," "family," "our people"
4. Use the pronouns: we, our, us — they activate unity processing
5. Ask for ADVICE (not opinions or expectations) — advice-giving puts people in a merging/togetherness mindset
**Defense:**
- Recognize when "we"-group loyalty is being exploited: labor unions protecting bad actors, corporations using "we" language in codes of conduct to reduce surveillance expectations
- Three organizational countermeasures: (1) recognize that corrupt actors presume "we"-group protection, (2) announce no tolerance in THIS group, (3) establish dismissal policy for proven abuses
- Beware of "blue lies" — lies told to protect the in-group that members view as morally superior to truth-telling
- Written commitment to ethical values (your "sign in the window") can resist in-group pressure to compromise
**Reversal:**
- Unity claims that feel manufactured or pandering create suspicion and push people away
- Exploiting in-group loyalty for personal gain (affinity fraud) causes catastrophic trust destruction when exposed
- Fervent group identity can corrupt: political partisans hide tax fraud evidence for own-party politicians, believe own party made stronger contributions (when identical), rank kidney patients by party affiliation

### [INF:UN.1] Kinship — Belonging Together via Family
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** Being in the same family is the ultimate form of self-other unity. People are particularly willing to help genetically close relatives, especially in survival-related decisions. Brain-imaging: people experience unusually high self-reward-center stimulation after aiding family. Physical and attitudinal similarities serve as cues of genetic overlap — we use them to judge (fairly accurately) relatedness to strangers.
**Key Examples:**
- Cialdini's survey: students sent questionnaire to parents for 1 test point → 97% response rate. "As an influence researcher, I've never experienced anything like it."
- Joel Stein's grandmother: voted for his candidate not because she understood his arguments, but "because her grandson wanted her to."
- Sugihara: saw parents care for diverse impoverished guests in their home → later risked career to save thousands of Jews.
- Mother Teresa: watched her mother Drabna feed, clothe, and house strangers. When asked who they were: "They are our people."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Use familial framing: "I will tell you what I would say to my family" (Buffett)
2. For parents: invite cross-group children into the home and treat them as family, not guests — assign chores, provide care
3. The "fictive family" effect: using kin language in organizations produces self-sacrifice normally seen only in actual families

### [INF:UN.2] Place — Belonging Together via Geography
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** Perceiving someone as from the same place — home, locality, region — creates "we"-ness. This operates at every scale from hometown to continent. "Localism" is a recognized phenomenon with enormous behavioral impact.
**Key Examples:**
- Nazi guard killing every 10th prisoner — skipped one who was from his hometown and killed the 11th instead.
- Holocaust rescuers: most were not approached by victims directly but by their OWN relatives or neighbors on behalf of the victims. Trocmé leveraged existing unities in Le Chambon.
- Obama 2008: 700+ local field offices with door-to-door canvassing by LOCAL volunteers → won three contested states and turned electoral toss-up into blowout.
- Ghana taxi drivers: lower fare for same-party passengers — but only during election season (when identity is salient).

### [INF:UN.3] Acting Together — Synchrony and Cooperation
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** Acting together in unison or coordination creates feelings of unity equivalent to belonging together. This includes marching, singing, reading aloud, tapping, sipping water in sync — even brain waves begin to match. The effect is System 1 (emotional, intuitive), not System 2 (rational, deliberative). Music is the most powerful vehicle for synchronous action.
**Key Examples:**
- Marching in step: team members 50% more cooperative toward teammates afterward (vs walking normally together).
- 4-year-olds: those who sang and walked in time with music were 3x more likely to help their partner afterward — self-sacrificially giving up play time.
- White subjects synchronized water-sipping with Black actors → eliminated typical White-favoring racial bias.
- Professor Wosinska's Soviet Poland memory: celebrating with songs and flags in unison — "Never has she felt more impelled to 'All for one, and one for all.'"
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Create shared rhythmic experiences: team songs, coordinated activities, joint music
2. For team-building: physically demanding shared experiences (mud runs, obstacle courses) bond through joint suffering
3. Match System 1 vs System 2: for emotional products, use music and synchrony; for rational products, use facts and logic

### [INF:UN.4] Co-creation and Asking for Advice
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** People who co-create something with you feel merged identity and become more supportive. The IKEA effect (valuing what you built) extends to CO-creation with another — you value both the creation AND the co-creator. Asking for ADVICE (not opinions or expectations) puts people in a togetherness/merging mindset, increasing their engagement and support.
**Key Examples:**
- Aldo Leopold cut down a birch tree to help a pine he had PLANTED — "My bias is paternal."
- Cialdini's manager study: managers with perceived involvement rated the ad 50% more favorably AND attributed success more to both themselves AND their employee (defying distributional logic).
- Splash! restaurant study: people asked for "advice" wanted to eat there MORE than those asked for "opinions" or "expectations" — despite rating all three types of feedback as equally helpful.
- Saul Bellow: "When we ask for advice, we are usually looking for an accomplice."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Involve stakeholders in creating the thing you want them to support
2. Ask for ADVICE, not opinions or expectations — it creates a togetherness mindset
3. Frame co-creation as genuine collaboration, not token involvement
4. Brands: invite customers to co-create products and services — they become more loyal

### [INF:UN.5] Suffering Together
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** Shared pain is a bonding agent — "he today that sheds his blood with me, shall be my brother" (Henry V). Joint adversity creates feelings of "we"-ness stronger than joint pleasant experiences. This explains boot camp, fraternity hazing, and the bond between crisis survivors.
**Key Examples:**
- Hitler protected a Jewish judge, Ernst Hess, from the Holocaust — because they had suffered together and been wounded within 24 hours of each other in the Somme offensive of WWI. The order came from the Fuhrer himself.
- Boston Marathon bombing 2013: residents with direct involvement became more fused with the Boston community.
- Ice-water experiment: groups that submerged hands in ice water together became more bonded and more willing to sacrifice personal gain for group welfare than groups using room-temperature water.
- Choctaw Nation → Ireland: Choctaws sent $170 during Ireland's Great Famine (1847), having just endured the Trail of Tears. In 2020, the Irish sent hundreds of thousands to Navajos during COVID.
### [INF:UN.6] Reciprocal Self-Disclosure
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** Extended reciprocal exchange of personal information creates unity bonds powerful enough to generate love in 45 minutes. Aron & Aron's 36-question procedure: partners take turns answering increasingly personal questions. Some participants married as a result. Two key features: escalating disclosiveness AND coordinated back-and-forth action.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. In any relationship you want to deepen: share something personal and invite reciprocation
2. Escalate gradually — early questions light, later questions more personal
3. The turn-taking structure itself creates synchrony and unity
4. Works cross-group: limited self-disclosure deepens cross-group relationships and reduces prejudice
