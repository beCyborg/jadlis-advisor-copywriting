# Influence: Application Patterns Reference

Source: Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion, Robert Cialdini (2021 expanded edition)

---

## Cross-Principle Combinations

### The Meta-Goal Model (Neidert's Core Motives)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Introduction
**Core Principle:** Cialdini's 2021 edition sequences the 7 principles by persuasive goal. Deploy them in this order for maximum effect:

1. **Cultivating a Positive Relationship** → `[INF:RC]` Reciprocation + `[INF:LK]` Liking + `[INF:UN]` Unity
2. **Reducing Uncertainty** → `[INF:SP]` Social Proof + `[INF:AU]` Authority
3. **Motivating Action** → `[INF:CC]` Commitment & Consistency + `[INF:SC]` Scarcity

**Key Insight:** Deploying principles out of order is a common failure mode. Scarcity pressure without relationship trust = pushy. Authority claims without liking = cold expertise. Social proof without relationship context = "so what?" The sequence matters.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. First: establish rapport and goodwill (give first, find similarities, invoke shared identity)
2. Then: reduce doubt (show social proof, establish expertise and trustworthiness)
3. Finally: motivate immediate action (activate prior commitments, create urgency through scarcity)

### Reciprocity + Authority Stack
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** The UK banker charity study demonstrated the multiplicative power of combining principles. CEO letter (authority alone) = 12%. Sweets gift (reciprocity alone) = 11%. CEO letter + sweets = 17%. The combination exceeded either principle alone.
**Practical Pattern:** Give something of value (activating reciprocity) from a credible source (activating authority). Example: an expert sharing exclusive research for free before making a request.

### Reciprocity + Credible Authority (Vincent the Waiter)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Vincent combined multiple principles in a "single, elegant maneuver." He recommended LESS expensive dishes (seeming to argue against his own interest), which established both trustworthiness and reciprocity (he did the customer a favor). Then he upsold wine and desserts with enormous credibility.
**Pattern:** Sacrifice a small gain early to establish credibility → then recommend the high-margin items with earned trust.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Recommend something CHEAPER or acknowledge a weakness first
2. This establishes you as trustworthy (authority) and as doing the customer a favor (reciprocity)
3. Then make your real recommendation — it will be believed more deeply
4. Works in sales, consulting, and any advisory relationship

### Social Proof + Similarity (Peer-suasion)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** Social proof from SIMILAR others is exponentially more powerful than from dissimilar ones. This is "peer-suasion." The Bible salesman's 119.67% improvement came from matching the gender/status of testimonial givers to the prospect. Chris learned to swim not from his father or a swimming instructor but from three-year-old Tommy.
**Pattern:** Always match the social proof source to the target audience. Female testimonials for female prospects, peers for peers, same-age for same-age.

### Scarcity + Social Proof (Competition)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Scarcity caused by SOCIAL DEMAND is more powerful than scarcity by accident. In the cookie study, cookies made scarce because of demand were rated highest of all. This combines scarcity (less available) with social proof (others want it too).
**Pattern:** "Due to high demand, we only have X left" is stronger than "We only have X left." Making competition VISIBLE (waiting lists, real-time viewer counts, "X people looking at this now") activates both principles simultaneously.

### Commitment + Scarcity (Low-Ball)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** Get the commitment first with a favorable offer, then the commitment "grows its own legs" even after the favorable offer is withdrawn. This is the low-ball technique — it works because new justifications sprout to support the choice.
**Pattern:** The Iowa energy conservation study: homeowners committed to save energy for newspaper publicity → publicity removed → they saved even MORE. The low-ball built genuine internal commitment that exceeded the original inducement.
**Ethical Application:** Use the low-ball structure for pro-social goals — get initial commitment through any available inducement, then remove it once self-sustaining motivation develops.

### Unity + Social Proof (In-Group Conformity)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** Social proof from in-group members is the strongest possible form. People follow the preferences and actions of fellow "we"-group members reflexively. Political party members trust co-partisans' judgment on irrelevant tasks, even when the co-partisan performs poorly.
**Pattern:** When deploying social proof, identify the audience's most salient in-group identity and show that OTHER IN-GROUP MEMBERS have already adopted the desired behavior.

### Liking + Unity (Distinction)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapters 3 & 8
**Core Principle:** Liking says "that person is like us" — it works through similarity, attractiveness, and familiarity. Unity says "that person is one OF us" — it works through shared identity. Unity is deeper and more powerful. Ali Reda surpassed Joe Girard because he had ethnic "we"-ness, not just liking.
**Pattern:** If you can invoke unity (shared identity), it supersedes liking. If you can't, liking is still powerful. Don't confuse the two — they operate at different psychological levels.

### The Tupperware Stack (All Principles)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** The Tupperware party is a masterclass in stacking all 7 principles:
- `[INF:RC]` Reciprocation: games, prizes, grab bags before buying
- `[INF:AU]` Authority: product quality certified by experts
- `[INF:SP]` Social Proof: each purchase signals to others
- `[INF:SC]` Scarcity: unique benefits, limited-time offers
- `[INF:CC]` Commitment: public descriptions of Tupperware benefits they already enjoy
- `[INF:UN]` Unity: buyers welcomed to "the Tupperware family"
- `[INF:LK]` Liking: the hostess is a FRIEND, not a salesperson
**Result:** $5.5 million in daily sales worldwide.

## The Contrast Principle (Meta-Tool)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** If the second item is fairly different from the first, we see it as being MORE different than it actually is. This is not one of the 7 principles but a perceptual amplifier that enhances them. The contrast principle is "virtually undetectable" in use.
**Key Examples:**
- Real estate "setup" properties: show undesirable houses first, then the real listing looks much better.
- Retail clothing: sell the suit ($800) first, then the sweater ($95) seems trivial by comparison. Reverse order makes the sweater seem more expensive.
- Car dealers: negotiate the $30,000 price first, then add $200 options one at a time — each seems petty by comparison.
- Sharon's letter to parents: describes skull fracture, pregnancy, boyfriend with infection... then reveals the "D" and "F" grades "in their proper perspective."
- Airline overbook: desk agent joked about $10,000 voucher, then revealed real $200 offer — it seemed like a pittance. Should have started with a joke offer of $2.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Present the expensive/large/extreme item FIRST, then the target item seems smaller
2. In negotiations: anchor high (but not absurdly so), then each concession seems more reasonable
3. In rejection-then-retreat `[INF:RC.3]`: the contrast principle ADDS to reciprocity — the smaller request looks even smaller by comparison
4. In pricing: show the premium tier first, then the standard tier looks like a bargain

## Defense Mechanisms — Master Guide

### The Universal Defense Pattern
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), All Defense Sections + Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** Each principle works as a mental shortcut — a "click, run" response to a trigger feature. The shortcuts are usually correct and save cognitive resources. The enemy is not the shortcut but those who COUNTERFEIT the trigger. Defense is NOT about rejecting the principles (that would make life unmanageable) — it's about identifying when the trigger is fake.

**Cialdini's Test for Exploitation:** Is the trigger feature a NATURAL part of the situation, or has it been FABRICATED by the compliance practitioner? Natural = legitimate use. Fabricated = exploitation.

### Defense by Principle

| Principle | Detection Signal | Counter-Maneuver |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| `[INF:RC]` Reciprocation | Feeling obligated after receiving something you didn't request | Redefine: "This was a sales tactic, not a gift." Reciprocity applies to favors, not to tricks |
| `[INF:LK]` Liking | Liking someone more than expected in a compliance situation | Mentally separate the PERSON from the OFFER. Focus on deal merits |
| `[INF:SP]` Social Proof | Following the crowd without independent assessment | Check for fabricated evidence: canned laughter, fake reviews, seeded tip jars |
| `[INF:AU]` Authority | Deferring to titles/uniforms without questioning substance | Ask: "Is this authority truly an expert?" AND "How truthful can I expect this expert to be?" |
| `[INF:SC]` Scarcity | Rush of arousal, urgency, fear of missing out | Use the arousal as your WARNING signal. Ask: "Do I want this for owning or using?" |
| `[INF:CC]` Consistency | Stomach-pit feeling of being trapped by prior words | Stomach sign: call it out. Heart-of-hearts sign: "Would I make the same choice again?" |
| `[INF:UN]` Unity | In-group loyalty overriding ethical judgment | Demand no-tolerance policies. Written commitment to ethical values as "sign in the window" |

### The Ethical Bright Line
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** Compliance professionals who play FAIRLY by the rules of shortcut responding are our ALLIES, not adversaries. The enemy is only those who FALSIFY, COUNTERFEIT, or MISREPRESENT the evidence. We should PROMOTE those who arm us with legitimate influence information and DEMOTE those who try to harm us with fabricated triggers.
**Cialdini's Call to Action:** "We should be willing to use shame, threat, confrontation, censure, tirade, nearly anything, to retaliate" against those who counterfeit influence triggers — because they threaten the reliability of our essential mental shortcuts.

### Brad the TV Salesman Test
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 9 (Reader's Report 9.1)
**Pattern:** Even Cialdini himself bought a TV after Brad used scarcity ("last one" + "another buyer called"). Was he a fool? It depends. Cialdini returned next day — no replacement on the shelf. Brad had been honest. Therefore Brad was a valuable informant, not a profiteer, and deserved a positive review. Had Brad lied, the review would have been a condemnation.
**Framework:** After any influence encounter, verify whether the trigger was genuine. Reward honest use; punish fabrication.

## Instant Influence — Modern Applications
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 9

### The Click-Run Paradigm
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapters 1 & 9
**Core Principle:** Most human behavior operates on automatic "click, run" shortcuts — just like animal fixed-action patterns triggered by single stimulus features (turkey mother responding to "cheep-cheep" regardless of source). We rely on these shortcuts because modern life is too complex, fast-paced, and information-dense for fully considered analysis of every decision. The 7 principles ARE these shortcuts — they usually point us to correct behavior. The problem: they can be triggered by counterfeit stimuli.
**Key Examples:**
- Langer's copy machine study: "Can I use the Xerox machine because I have to make some copies?" (no real reason given) = 93% compliance, nearly identical to providing a real reason (94%). The word "because" alone triggered compliance.
- "Expensive = good" stereotype: turquoise jewelry sold at DOUBLE the price; energy drink at $1.89 worked better than at $0.89; pain reliever at $2.50 rated more effective than at $0.10. Same products.
- Captainitis in aviation: copilots defer to captains even when the captain makes an obvious error that kills everyone.

### The Big Mistake (Anti-Pattern)
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** The most common error in social-influence campaigns is publicizing the FREQUENCY of undesirable behavior. This normalizes it via social proof instead of discouraging it.
**Key Examples:**
- Petrified Forest: sign saying "14 tons stolen per year" TRIPLED theft to 7.92%. Sign depicting lone thief (marginalizing the behavior) reduced theft to 1.67%.
- US Forest Service "Gross National Product": bemoaning litter rates → implicitly validated littering.
- Eating-disorder education program: students who heard descriptions of disorders developed MORE symptoms.
- Suicide-prevention program: informing teens of "alarming" adolescent suicide rates → teens became MORE likely to see suicide as a solution.
**Counter-Pattern:** Always frame the POSITIVE majority behavior as the norm. "97% of visitors leave the forest intact for future generations" is far more effective than "14 tons are stolen annually."

### The Future Social Proof Technique
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** If you lack current social proof (new product, startup), use evidence of FUTURE social proof: "trending," "fastest growing," "projected to be." Research indicates this can be at least as effective as existing social proof.
**Pattern:** For startups and new products without adoption numbers, frame the trajectory: "joining the X fastest-growing companies in..." or "the trend toward X is accelerating."

### System 1 vs System 2 Matching
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** Persuaders should match the System 1 (emotional, intuitive) vs System 2 (rational, analytical) orientation of their appeal to the recipient's current mindset. Music and synchrony activate System 1; facts and arguments activate System 2.
**Key Examples:**
- "I FEEL this is the one for you" works for emotional decisions (attractive car). "I THINK this is the one for you" works for rational decisions (fuel economy).
- 87% of TV commercials use music — but research shows music only helps for familiar, feelings-based products. For high-consequence rational products, music HURTS effectiveness.
- Voltaire: "Anything too stupid to be spoken is sung." Advertising adage: "If you can't make your case with facts, sing it to them."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Assess the target's processing mode: are they in a feeling-based or thinking-based state?
2. For System 1 (hedonic, familiar products): use music, emotion, harmony, synchrony, stories
3. For System 2 (consequential, unfamiliar decisions): use facts, data, logic, expert analysis
4. Mismatching System 1 appeal to System 2 audience (or vice versa) reduces effectiveness

### E-Commerce Influence Stack
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), eBox 6.1
**Core Principle:** A review of 6,700+ A/B tests on e-commerce sites found the top-6 most effective features all grounded in behavioral psychology, in this order:
1. **Scarcity** `[INF:SC]` — highlighting items low in stock
2. **Social Proof** `[INF:SP]` — most popular and trending items
3. **Urgency** `[INF:SC.2]` — time limits with countdown timers
4. **Concessions** `[INF:RC]` — discounts for visitors to stay on the site
5. **Authority/Expertise** `[INF:AU]` — informing of alternative products available
6. **Liking** `[INF:LK]` — welcoming message
**Key Insight:** Limited-supply appeals outperform limited-time appeals in online settings. Both are top-3 factors.

### Online Reviews and Trust
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), eBox 5.2
**Principles in Play:** `[INF:SP]` + `[INF:AU.2]`
**Key Findings from Spiegel Research Center:**
- Five stars is TOO good — optimal rating is 4.2-4.7 range. Above that, buyers suspect fakes.
- Negative reviews INCREASE credibility — conversion jumps 67% with some negative reviews present.
- Verified buyers are gold — confirmed purchasers are viewed as more credible than anonymous reviewers.
- Reviewer who confesses a past purchase mistake is rated MORE trustworthy (same mechanism as Buffett/Vincent).
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Don't pursue a perfect 5-star rating — aim for 4.2-4.7
2. Don't panic about negative reviews — they establish credibility
3. Prioritize verified-buyer reviews over anonymous ones
4. If a reviewer mentions a past mistake, that review is MORE persuasive, not less

### The Werther Effect — Contagious Behavior
**Source:** Influence (Cialdini), Chapter 4
**Principles in Play:** `[INF:SP]` + `[INF:SP.2]` (similarity)
**Core Pattern:** Publicized suicides increase suicide rate by average 58 extra deaths within two months. The effect is specific: young victim → young copycats; older victim → older copycats. Single-victim suicides → single-fatality crashes; murder-suicides → multi-fatality crashes. The same pattern applies to mass shootings: "Going postal" wasn't about postal-office stress; it was peer-suasion among similarly situated people.
**Implication for Media:** Sensationalizing suicides and mass killings has measurable lethal consequences. Netflix's "13 Reasons Why" caused 28.9% spike in youth suicides in the following month.
**Implication for Communicators:** NEVER highlight the frequency of harmful behavior you want to reduce. Instead, spotlight the positive majority.

## Situational Quick-Reference

### For Sales & Marketing
1. Give something valuable FIRST `[INF:RC]`
2. Find genuine similarities with the customer `[INF:LK.2]`
3. Show popularity ("most popular," reviews, adoption numbers) `[INF:SP]`
4. Establish credibility by acknowledging a weakness first `[INF:AU.2]`
5. Show limited availability `[INF:SC.1]`
6. Get a small commitment early `[INF:CC.1]`

### For Negotiations
1. Open with a concession or gift `[INF:RC]`
2. Find shared interests early ("Oh, you're a runner too!") `[INF:LK.2]`
3. Use rejection-then-retreat: start high, concede to real target `[INF:RC.3]`
4. Show that similar parties have agreed to comparable terms `[INF:SP.2]`
5. Cite expert/authority support for your position `[INF:AU]`
6. Frame as potential LOSS, not potential gain `[INF:SC]`
7. Invoke shared identity or partnership `[INF:UN]`

### For Behavior Change (Personal/Organizational)
1. Get a written, public commitment to the desired behavior `[INF:CC.2]`
2. Start with small commitments and build `[INF:CC.1]`
3. Use reminders of past commitments `[INF:CC.6]`
4. Show that peers are already doing it `[INF:SP.2]`
5. Remove external rewards so internal motivation develops `[INF:CC.4]`
6. Create shared experiences around the desired behavior `[INF:UN.3]`

### For Defense Against Manipulation
1. Recognize the "click, run" moment — when you're acting without thinking
2. Identify which principle is being triggered
3. Apply Cialdini's Test: is the trigger natural or fabricated?
4. If natural: the practitioner is an ally — the shortcut is valid
5. If fabricated: redefine the situation ("This is a tactic, not a gift") and retaliate
6. Use principle-specific defenses (see Defense by Principle table above)
