# Great Leads — Michael Masterson & John Forde  ·  prefix [GL]
> The first 100–600 words of a sales message are a system: pick the lead type by how much the prospect already knows, unify it under one idea. Use this lens whenever the decision is "how do I open?" — headline + opening paragraphs of any promo, landing page, email, or VSL.

This file is the strategic layer: the six lead archetypes as an awareness-driven matrix (type → who it fits → skeleton → how it breaks) plus the three framing principles (Rule of One, the Awareness Scale, Direct vs Indirect). The tactical layer — physical opener templates and EN/RU few-shots — lives in `copy-patterns.md`.

## Master matrix
Awareness runs most→least aware: Most Aware · Product-Aware · Solution-Aware · Problem-Aware · Unaware. Directness tracks it: the more aware the prospect, the more directly you may open.

| Code | Lead | Awareness fit | Directness | Opener skeleton | When it fails |
|------|------|---------------|-----------|-----------------|---------------|
| [GL:OFFER] | Offer | Most Aware | Most direct | Deal detail → biggest deal-benefit → elaborate → "reason why" | On less-aware readers it feels pushy and easy to dismiss; on jaded-but-aware buyers (car-lot/insurance reflex) high awareness turns negative; heavy freebies on a high-value product cheapen it |
| [GL:PROMISE] | Promise | Product-Aware / on-the-fence | Direct | Single biggest benefit up front → tie to core desire → proof | Bounces off skeptics and the "hyper-aware" (the millionth benefit head → "yeah, right"); claim too big to believe; wasted on the unaware who lack context |
| [GL:PROBSOL] | Problem-Solution | Solution-Aware & Problem-Aware | Semi-direct | Name the sharpest worry → prove empathy → offer hope of a relevant fix | On the most-aware it wastes their time building empathy they don't need; lingering on the problem too long; "going negative" on a prospect who isn't problem-focused |
| [GL:SECRET] | Big Secret | Problem-/Solution-Aware, skeptical, seen-it-all | Indirect | Name+withhold a secret in the headline → drip clues → reveal only when they're ready (often only on purchase) | Disclosing the secret inside the lead (curiosity satisfied → they quit); secret not tied to a real benefit; an anticlimactic reveal that under-delivers on the tease |
| [GL:PROCLAIM] | Proclamation | Unaware (also aware B2B if startling) | Indirect | Bold fact/prediction → prove it → bridge to the implicit promise | An ordinary statement is skipped; a claim too incredible breeds disbelief; no clean bridge from the jolt back to the product |
| [GL:STORY] | Story | Unaware / skeptical; most flexible | Most indirect (can flex) | Open mid-conflict on a relatable hero → resolve → keep the thread to the product taut | A dull or overlong story; the Golden Thread goes slack (entertaining but doesn't sell — the product isn't fused to the tale); told too slowly |

Rule of thumb from the authors' own tally: ~80% of winning packages lead with one of these six pure types (or a tight blend of two similar ones). Blend only after you can execute each cleanly, and only if the blend still serves one core message ([GL:ONE]).

## Principles

### [GL:ONE] The Rule of One  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** A great lead carries exactly one big idea, wrapped in one dominant emotion, and drives to one action. Extra ideas don't add power — they split the emotional charge and cancel each other out ("tossed-salad" copy).
- **Copy move:** Before writing, name the single idea the whole promo exists to plant. Then assemble five parts around it: (1) one good idea, (2) one core emotion it triggers, (3) one captivating story or fact that proves it, (4) one single desirable benefit, (5) one inevitable next action. Cut anything that serves a *second* idea.
- **Applies to:** short-form | landing | email | VSL — every lead type below is still governed by it.
- **Applies / fails when:** Governs all copy. The one caveat: Offer and Promise elements may co-exist with another lead type because a deal/claim is *indispensable* to every package — you're surfacing it, not adding a competing idea. A genuine second *idea* still dilutes.
- **Few-shot (EN):** Coke ran "The pause that refreshes" OR "Always Cool" — never both fused. Negative: "Learn Spanish fast, boost your memory, AND meet new people" — three ideas, three emotions, zero pull.
- **Few-shot (RU):** Онлайн-школа английского: «Заговоришь через 3 месяца» ИЛИ «Английский без зубрёжки грамматики» — одна идея на лендинг, не обе сразу. Негатив: «Английский быстро, плюс прокачаешь память И найдёшь друзей по переписке» — три идеи, три эмоции, ноль тяги.
- **Book ref:** Masterson noticed his highest-rated newsletter issues each argued a single point; Ogilvy had long called the same thing "the Big Idea." Both concluded one clear idea beats a dozen strung together.

### [GL:AWARE] The Awareness Scale  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** The decisive question before writing is *"What does the prospect already know?"* — about you, your product, and his own problem. Gene Schwartz's five levels, most→least aware: Most Aware (knows product, wants the deal) · Product-Aware (knows product, unsure it's right) · Solution-Aware (wants the outcome, doesn't know your product delivers it) · Problem-Aware (feels the pain, doesn't know a fix exists) · Unaware (no knowledge but his own identity/angst).
- **Copy move:** Plot the target list on the scale first; the level dictates where the headline starts — at the product, at the desire, or at the problem. Diagnose it from the list source (house multi-buyers = most aware; cold prospecting = less aware) and from product novelty (brand-new = low awareness).
- **Applies to:** every channel; it is the routing switch for all six lead types.
- **Applies / fails when:** Reliable as a first cut; not absolute — always test. Watch the edge case: "over-aware / jaded" buyers sit high on the scale but have made up their minds, so treat them as skeptics, not easy sells.
- **Few-shot (EN):** Same tooth-whitening product: to a repeat customer, open with the discount (most aware); to someone who only knows "my teeth are yellow and I hate it," open on that feeling (problem-aware).
- **Few-shot (RU):** Один и тот же сервис доставки готовых рационов: постоянному подписчику открывай скидкой на продление (most aware); тому, кто знает лишь «вечно нет времени готовить, а третья пицца за неделю уже стыдно» — открывай этим чувством (problem-aware).
- **Book ref:** Schwartz's rule, paraphrased: if the prospect knows the product, start there; if he knows only the desire, start with the desire; if only the problem, start there and crystallize it into a need.

### [GL:DIRIND] Direct vs Indirect  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Directness = how fast the copy names the product and the deal. Direct leads (Offer, Promise) suit aware, ready buyers; indirect leads (Story, Secret, Proclamation, and the semi-direct Problem-Solution) suit less-aware or skeptical readers who need the awareness gap closed before you reveal you're selling.
- **Copy move:** Choose directness off the Awareness Scale. Go direct when the product is easy to grasp, the promise is large and believable, the deal is strong, or the reader already trusts you. Go indirect when the reader barely knows you, the product needs explaining, skepticism is high, there's a timely news hook, or you're re-inventing the offer.
- **Applies to:** landing | VSL | email | short-form.
- **Applies / fails when:** Indirect can outperform enormously but is higher-risk and harder to write — the failure modes are being too subtle, too slow, or "interesting but irrelevant" (clever brand humor that never sells). Direct fails by coming on too strong and letting the reader pre-decide "not for me."
- **Few-shot (EN):** A logged-in SaaS user who churned last month → direct win-back offer. A cold reader who's never heard the category exists → an indirect story or proclamation to earn the first minute.
- **Few-shot (RU):** Клиент SaaS для онлайн-записи, отписавшийся месяц назад → прямой оффер-возврат со скидкой на первый месяц. Холодный читатель, впервые слышащий, что «журнал записи клиентов можно автоматизировать» → непрямой заход через историю или проклэйм, чтобы заслужить первую минуту внимания.
- **Book ref:** Bonner's war analogy, paraphrased: empires win head-on when they're strong, but the outmatched win by flanking. You're not warring on the customer — you're warring on years of built-up ad-resistance, and sometimes the indirect route is the only way in.

### [GL:OFFER] Offer Lead  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** The most direct lead — it lifts the curtain on the deal (price, discount, premium, guarantee) right up front, often in the headline. Easiest to write *when the deal is strong and the audience is warm*, because a good deal to a ready buyer is hard to botch.
- **Copy move:** Four steps: (1) open on the most emotionally-compelling detail of the offer, (2) underscore its most valuable benefit, (3) elaborate that deal-benefit, (4) supply a "reason why" the deal exists (scarcity, deadline, news) to defuse "what's the catch?". Sub-type: the **Invitation** offer, which trades explicit price for exclusivity ("you are cordially invited…") — works only when the thing really is exclusive.
- **Applies to:** email to house lists | landing for warm traffic | renewal/upsell.
- **Applies / fails when:** Best for Most-Aware buyers. Fails when awareness is high but so is skepticism (the car-lot/insurance reflex) — then go indirect to rebuild trust. A "FREE"-heavy offer on a high-perceived-value product backfires: freebies make the paid product look cheap.
- **Book ref:** The Economist's "May I send you 3 FREE issues…" opened straight on the deal and leaned on exclusivity — but only worked because years of brand credibility preceded it; to a cold list the free issues would have gone over like free ice cream in winter.

### [GL:PROMISE] Promise Lead  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** The most common lead — slightly less direct than the Offer (product named a beat later), it opens with the product's single biggest, boldest claim. The promise is the headline, the first line, often the last line too.
- **Copy move:** Find the promise via Reeves' USP: it must (1) come from what the product genuinely does, (2) be *unique* — something competitors can't/won't say, (3) target a desire the prospect *already has* (you can't create desire, only awaken it). Push past the functional benefit to the emotional one (status, safety, how they'll be seen). Keep the claim bold *but believable* — scale it down until it's provable; back every promise with proof.
- **Applies to:** landing | email | VSL for warm-to-lukewarm traffic.
- **Applies / fails when:** Best for Product-Aware / on-the-fence prospects who need one more nudge. Fails on skeptics and the "hyper-aware" who've seen the same promise a thousand times, and on the fully unaware who lack the context to value the claim. An oversized claim that under-delivers only makes the product fail faster.
- **Book ref:** "Get Rich Slow" beat the sea of "get rich quick" claims by scaling the promise into the reader's comfort zone; the Sears DieHard demo believed only after they cut it from 10 cars to 3 — smaller claim, credible claim.

### [GL:PROBSOL] Problem-Solution Lead  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** The "hot-button" classic — delay the product and open by naming the prospect's biggest, most emotionally-charged worry, proving "I feel your pain" before pivoting to "…and I have a solution." Naming the unspoken worry builds trust a direct claim can't.
- **Copy move:** Five beats: (1) target what keeps them up at night, (2) make sure it carries deep emotional weight, (3) stir that emotion first to prove empathy, (4) don't linger on the problem longer than it takes to win the read-on, (5) offer hope of a relevant solution. Mine the real worry by *listening* to prospects (emails, forums, calls), hunting the deeper "core emotion" beneath the surface problem.
- **Applies to:** landing | VSL | long email; especially health, finance, self-improvement.
- **Applies / fails when:** Best for Solution-Aware and Problem-Aware readers. Fails on the Most-Aware (empathy-building wastes their time), when you dwell on the negative too long, or when the prospect isn't actually problem-focused (uninvited "bad news" repels). Format variants: If-Then, Inversion/Negative-Promise, Identification, The Question, Instruction (invented term) — see `copy-patterns.md`.
- **Book ref:** Kleenex found its billion-dollar footing not as "movie-star skin care" but as "Don't Put a Cold In Your Pocket" — the disposable-handkerchief pitch that named the cold/hay-fever sufferer's actual daily misery.

### [GL:SECRET] Big Secret Lead  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** The "velvet pouch" — tease hard-to-come-by knowledge, a formula, or a system in the headline and *withhold the reveal* while you build desire. Taps the deep human belief that success has a secret; whoever promises to reveal it commands attention.
- **Copy move:** Introduce an intriguing, benefit-bearing secret in the headline; do NOT disclose it in the lead (100–600 words); drip clues that feel like progress but reveal nothing. Two ways to source the secret: find one genuinely inside the product (an unusual ingredient/method), or **neologize/transubstantiate** an ordinary feature — rename dull "dividend stocks" as the "Chaffee Royalty Program" so it feels new and secret. Best mileage: the full reveal comes only *with the purchased product*.
- **Applies to:** VSL | long landing | direct-mail-style email; dominant in info-products, supplements, finance, systems.
- **Applies / fails when:** Best for skeptical, seen-it-all readers (and aware financial buyers). Fails the instant you disclose too soon — the #1 beginner mistake; the satisfied reader stops. Fails if the secret isn't tied to a major benefit, or if the eventual reveal is anticlimactic versus the tease.
- **Book ref:** The "Chaffee Royalty Program" letter kept readers hunting through pages of celebrity-royalty examples before admitting the "secret" was a dividend strategy — the invented name did all the work; the plain-English version would have stopped the reader cold.

### [GL:PROCLAIM] Proclamation Lead  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** Open with an emotionally-compelling assertion — a startling fact, a bold opinion, or a prediction — that jars the reader out of his seat, then prove it before revealing the pitch. Reads like a (sometimes tabloid) news story; borrows journalism's frame to lower ad-defenses.
- **Copy move:** Lead with a statement that startles, intrigues, AND tempts (it must carry an *implicit* promise). Push toward the edge of the incredible — ordinary statements get skipped — but stay relevant and provable. Hold the USP back until the proclamation is proven. For **predictions**, staple the claim to a specific date to spike credibility and urgency. Once the hook is set, you may switch to more direct techniques (even an invitation).
- **Applies to:** VSL | landing | space ads; political/health/financial/environmental ideas.
- **Applies / fails when:** Best for the Unaware (also works on aware B2B when the statement genuinely alarms them, e.g. "Your 1995 PDR is Obsolete"). Fails if the statement is ordinary, irrelevant, or unprovable, or if you can't bridge the jolt back to the product; too-incredible claims breed disbelief instead of curiosity.
- **Book ref:** "Read This Or Die" (a health "report-a-log") arrested readers with the claim that they had a 95% chance of dying from a *curable* disease — startle plus the tease of "known cures somewhere on the planet" — then spent pages proving it before the invitation to subscribe.

### [GL:STORY] Story Lead  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** The most indirect and, the authors argue, the most consistently powerful opener. A well-told story makes the reader forget he's reading an ad — his defenses drop and he "lives" the idea instead of being asked to accept it. It resolves the prospect's core ambivalence: he wants to buy but doesn't want to be sold.
- **Copy move:** Begin *in the middle* of a conflict affecting a protagonist the reader identifies with; carry it to an emotionally-satisfying resolution that implies the product's promise. Keep the **Golden Thread** taut: product at one end, prospect's heart at the other, every line fastened to both — if it goes slack for a sentence, the sale is lost. Tell it fast. Usually don't name the product in the headline (a common mistake) — but still fuse the story to it (Hathaway shirt was integral; Dos Equis fails because the man could drink anything).
- **Applies to:** landing | VSL | email | fundraising; travel, finance, self-improvement especially.
- **Applies / fails when:** Best for Unaware/skeptical readers; the most flexible type — can be played direct or indirect. Fails when the story is dull, overlong, slowly told, or entertaining-but-disconnected from the product.
- **Book ref:** The Wall Street Journal's "two young men" letter — identical graduates, one a manager, one the president 25 years later — never mentioned the paper or the offer in its opening yet ran as a control for ~28 years and an estimated $2B in revenue; Caples' "They laughed when I sat down at the piano…" told a full story in 15 words.
